### Method notes and caveats — read these before trusting a quote

- **All Edwards & Magee and Murphy quotes below are transcribed from the Internet Archive OCR of the editions named above.** The OCR contains occasional run-together words (`thatis`, `Itis`, `differin`), a stray `¬` at hyphenated line breaks in the Murphy scan, and mangled figure captions. Quotes are reproduced **as the OCR renders them**; where an OCR artifact sits inside a quote I have left it rather than silently "correcting" it, so that any number carries a checkable string. **Verify any quote you plan to hard-code against a print copy.**
- **The edition matters and is a real conflict source.** S2 is the *eleventh* edition, in which the editor W.H.C. Bassetti has interpolated commentary marked `EN:` / `EN9:` (editor's note). Where a criterion comes from Bassetti rather than from Edwards & Magee's own text I flag it. The 1948 Edwards & Magee did not contain, e.g., the "Kilroy Bottom" renaming or the "Spikes / Runaway Days / Key Reversal Days" section.
- **Murphy's book is a futures-first text retitled for all markets.** Several rules carry futures carve-outs (the 3% rule explicitly does not apply to interest-rate futures). Applying them to a US equity screener is an extrapolation the book does not make.
- **Web search budget for this session was exhausted (200/200) after 4 searches**, so the brief was not met on search count. The searches that did run pointed at the two archive.org full texts, which is a strictly better outcome than 10 blog summaries — every substantive rule below comes from the books' own text rather than a reproduction. The Bulkowski corroboration was obtained by direct `WebFetch`, which was still available.
- **These two books almost never measure.** Across ~25 patterns, the total published, sample-backed statistics amount to: zero from Murphy, and from Edwards & Magee a handful of frankly-labelled estimates ("our estimate would be", "lacking an actual statistical count, our experience would suggest", "roughly estimated"). Not one carries a sample size, a date range, a universe, or a base rate. Wherever this file says "asserted", it means exactly that.

# PART A — MECHANICS (the rules both books insist on before any pattern is valid)

## Trendline construction and the number of touches

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 4 "Basic Concepts of Trend" → *Trendlines* / *Tentative Versus the Valid Trendline* / *How to Determine the Significance of a Trendline* / *Trendlines Should Include All Price Action*. Edwards & Magee, Ch. 14 "Trendlines and Channels" → *How Trendlines are drawn* / *Tests of authority*.
- **definition**: A straight line drawn across successive reaction lows (up trendline) or rally highs (down trendline). E&M explicitly declare that pattern boundaries are the same object: "The fact is that boundary lines of Triangles and Rectangles, as well as necklines of Head-and-Shoulders Formations, are simply special types of trendlines." Both books distinguish a *tentative* line (2 points) from a *valid/confirmed* line (3 points).
- **criteria**:
  - points required to draw a line — **2** — "A straight line is mathematically determined by any two points along it. To draw a trendline, therefore, we require two determining points—two established Top Reversal points to fix a Down Trendline and two established Bottom Reversal points to fix an Up Trendline." (E&M Ch.14) — confidence(high)
  - points required to validate a line — **3** — "In order to confirm the validity of a trendline, however, that line should be touched a third time with prices bouncing off of it. ... To summarize, two points are needed to draw the trendline, and a third point to make it a valid trendline." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
  - fourth touch strengthens it — **4** — "Should a fourth Bottom later form on it, and prices move up away from it again, its value as a trend criterion is very considerably enhanced." (E&M Ch.14) — confidence(high)
  - significance scales with touch count — value: null — "A trendline that has been successfully test¬ ed eight times, for example, that has continually demonstrated its validity, is obviously a more significant trendline than one that has only been touched three times." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(med) — **missing**: no threshold is published; "eight" is an illustration, not a rule. To be computable the book would have to publish a minimum touch count that changes the signal.
  - significance scales with duration — value: null — "a trendline that has been in effect for nine months is of more importance than one that has been in effect for nine weeks or nine days." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(med) — **missing**: no minimum duration published.
  - the two anchor pivots must be separated in time — **"less than a week apart" is called out as unreliable** — "If your trendline is drawn from two original Bottoms that are very close together in time—say, less than a week apart—it is subject to error; it may be too steep or (more often) too flat." (E&M Ch.14) — confidence(high)
  - touch count outranks duration — value: null (ordinal rule, no number) — "Greater weight should be given to the number of Bottoms that have formed on a trendline (Test 1) than to its length alone (Test 2)." (E&M Ch.14) — confidence(high)
  - line is drawn on the **full daily range**, not closes — value: null (a construction rule) — "Trendlines on bar charts should be drawn over or under the entire day's price range. Some chartists prefer to draw the trendline by connecting only the closing prices, but that is not the more stan¬ dard procedure." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
  - preferred slope — **≈45 degrees** — "In gen¬ eral, most important up trendlines tend to approximate an aver¬ age slope of 45 degrees." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(med) — note this is scale-dependent and therefore not reproducible without also fixing the chart aspect ratio; the book does not say so.
  - flatter is better (E&M) — value: null — "The flatter, more nearly horizontal the trendline, the more important it is technically" (E&M Ch.14, Test 3) — confidence(med) — **missing**: no angle threshold.
  - fan principle — **3rd line** — "The important point to remember here is that the breaking of the third line is the valid trend reversal signal." (Murphy Ch.4, *The Fan Principle*) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published. Neither book reports a hit rate for trendline signals over any sample. E&M's Ch.14 tests are labelled "tests of authority", i.e. judgement heuristics.
- **price_objective**: Murphy, Ch.4 *Measuring Implications of Trendlines*: "once a trendline is broken, prices will usually move a distance beyond the trendline equal to the vertical distance that prices achieved on the other side of the line, prior to the trend reversal." He then explicitly ties this to the H&S rule: "this measuring rule using the trendline is similar to that used for the well-known head and shoulders reversal pattern, where the distance from the 'head' to the 'neckline' is projected beyond that line once it's broken."
- **invalidation**: a close beyond the line satisfying the penetration filter (see next section). Murphy adds that a broken trendline reverses role: "an up trendline (a support line) will usually become a resistance line once it's deci¬ sively broken."
- **detection_notes**: Primitives — (1) pivot high / pivot low detection with explicit left/right bar parameters; (2) line fit through ≥2 pivots of the same type with a tolerance band; (3) a **touch counter** that counts subsequent bars whose low (up-line) or high (down-line) comes within tolerance of the line *and* that then move away from it — note both books require the *bounce*, not merely the touch, so a touch is a two-sided test (approach + departure) and cannot be computed from a single bar; (4) a time-separation guard on the two anchor pivots (E&M: reject anchors <1 week apart); (5) slope in *price units per bar*, which is NOT the "45 degrees" the book means — Murphy's 45° is a visual property of a rendered chart and is **not computable from OHLCV alone** without also fixing the pixel scaling; treat any 45° rule as unimplementable as stated. Everything else here is computable from daily OHLCV.
- **conflicts**: Murphy validates a line at the **third touch**; E&M validate at the third *bottom formed on it* and say a fourth "very considerably" enhances it — same direction, different emphasis. On slope they disagree in kind: **Murphy prescribes an absolute ~45° target**, while **E&M prescribe a relative preference ("the flatter... the more important")** and explicitly warn that "A very steep line can easily be broken by a brief sideways Consolidation move ... only to have prices shoot up again". Record both.

## The percentage penetration filter (the "3% rule") and the time filters (the "two-day rule")

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 4 → *What Constitutes a Valid Breaking of a Trendline?*; restated in Ch. 5 for the H&S neckline and again in Ch. 5 *Filters* for double tops. Edwards & Magee, Ch. 6 spec item D (H&S), Ch. 8 (triangles), Ch. 9 (rectangles, double/triple tops), Ch. 10 (broadening), Ch. 14 → *Validity of penetration*.
- **definition**: A price filter requires the close to exceed the broken line/level by a stated percentage. A time filter requires the close to hold beyond the line for a stated number of sessions.
- **criteria**:
  - **Murphy** — price filter, long-term trendlines — **3%, on a closing basis** — "One example of a price filter is the 3% penetration criteria. This price filter is used mainly for the breaking of longer term trend- lines, but requires that the trendline be broken, on a closing basis, by at least 3%." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — carve-out — **the 3% rule is not universal** — "(The 3% rule doesn't apply to some financial futures, such as the interest rate markets.)" — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — worked example — **$400 line ⇒ $388 close (12 points)** — "If, for example, gold prices broke a major up trendline at $400, prices would have to close below that line by 3% of the price level where the line was broken (in this case, prices would have to close $12 below the trendline, or at $388)." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — short-term substitute — **1%** — "Obviously, a $12 penetration criteria would not be appropriate for shorter term trading. Perhaps a 1% criterion would serve better in such cases." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — stock-chartist variant — **a full point** — "Stock chartists, for example, might require a full point penetration and ignore fractional moves." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — time filter — **two successive closes** — "A common time filter is the two day rule. In other words, to have a valid breaking of a trendline, prices must close beyond the trendline for two successive days. To break an up trendline, therefore, prices must close under the trendline two days in a row. A one day violation would not count." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — scope of both filters — **also applies to support/resistance, not just trendlines** — "The 1- 3% rule and the two day rule are also applied to the breaking of important support and resistance levels, not just to major trend- lines." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — weekly time filter — **a Friday close** — "Another filter would require a Friday close beyond a major breakout point to ensure a weekly signal." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — applied to the H&S neckline — **1–3% OR two successive closes** — "Here again the 1-3% penetration criterion (or some variation thereof) or the requirement of two successive closes below the neckline (the two day rule) can be used for added confirmation." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy** — applied to a double-top peak penetration — **1% or 3%, or two consecutive closes, or a Friday close** — "Second, a price filter of some type might be used. One such example is a percentage pene¬ tration criterion (such as 1% or 3%). Third, the two day penetration rule could be used as an example of a time filter. In other words, prices would have to close beyond the top of the first peak for two consecutive days to signal a valid penetration. Another time filter could be a Friday close beyond the previous peak." — confidence(high)
  - **Edwards & Magee** — the canonical figure — **3% of the stock's market price** (note: **of the price**, not of the pattern height) — "a close below that line by an amount approximately equivalent to 3% of the stock’s market price. This is the “confirmation” or “breakout.”" (Ch.6, H&S Top spec D) — confidence(high)
  - **Edwards & Magee** — the 3% need NOT be a single day — **may accumulate over 2–3 days** — "To be decisive, prices must not only push through the line but also close beyond it by a margin equal to about 3% of the stock’s price. This does not need to be accomplished in a single day, although it often is. The 3% penetration may come as a result of two or three days of gradual decline." (Ch.14) — confidence(high)
  - **Edwards & Magee** — volume can substitute for part of the margin — **2% + volume ≈ decisive** — "If, for example, prices start to decline from a point somewhat above the trendline, move down through it on conspicuously expanding turnover, and close beyond it, say, only 2% of the price but at or near the bottom of the day’s range, then our 3% margin rule has not been satisfied, but the lesser margin plus the volume action may be construed as decisive." (Ch.14) — confidence(high)
  - **Edwards & Magee** — indexes/averages get a tighter filter — **2%** — "Thus, although we ask for a 3% penetration in the case of an individual stock of medium range, 2% is ample in the Averages to give a dependable break signal." (Ch.15) — confidence(high)
  - **Edwards & Magee** — the same 3% is the *tolerance* for "same level" between two peaks — **3%; worked example 50 vs 51 1/2** — "Use here the 3% rule we have previously laid down as a measuring stick for breakouts. A first Top at 50, for example, and a second at 51 1/2 would come within this limit." (Ch.9, Double Tops) — confidence(high)
  - **Edwards & Magee** — the same 3% is the triangle breakout margin — **roughly 3%** — "For example, the margin by which prices should close beyond the pattern lines is the same, roughly 3%." (Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - **Edwards & Magee (editor, EN9)** — the break may mean *sideways*, not reversal — value: null — "ENS: In respect to the breaking of trendlines (by 2% or 3%), I should note the breaking of a trendline is as much a warning as a signal to act. The break, instead of a change of trend to the reverse, may indicate a change of trend to the sideways—into a reversal or continuation pattern." (Bassetti, Ch.15) — confidence(high) — this is **editor's commentary, not Edwards & Magee**.
- **measured_performance**: none published. Neither author reports a whipsaw-reduction rate, a sample, or a period for any filter. Murphy states the trade-off qualitatively and then explicitly declines to quantify: "There is tradeoff involved in the use of any type of filter. If the filter is too small, it won't be very useful in reduc¬ ing the impact of whipsaws. If it's too big, then much of the ini¬ tial move will be missed before a valid signal is given." And: "These filters are certainly not infallible, but do serve to reduce the number of false signals (or whipsaws) that often occur. Sometimes these filters are helpful, and sometimes they're not."
- **price_objective**: n/a (this is an entry filter, not a target rule).
- **invalidation**: a close back inside the pattern/line after a filtered break is treated by E&M as either a "false move" (trend then goes the other way) or a "premature breakout" (trend later resumes the same way) — and E&M state plainly these are **indistinguishable at the time**: "Both false moves and premature breakouts, in the sense in which we employ these terms, are indistinguishable at the time they occur from genuine breakouts."
- **detection_notes**: Fully computable from daily OHLCV. Primitives: (1) a level or fitted line evaluated at bar *t*; (2) `close[t]` vs `level[t] * (1 ± pct)` — **note the denominator: E&M normalise by the STOCK'S PRICE, Murphy by "the price level where the line was broken". These are the same number for a trendline break and different numbers for a neckline break far from current price. Pick one and record which.** (3) a consecutive-close counter for the two-day rule; (4) a weekday test for the Friday-close filter; (5) E&M's "accumulated over two or three days" variant needs a rolling window that asks whether *any* close within the last 3 bars is ≥3% beyond while all intervening closes stayed beyond — a materially different predicate from "a single close ≥3% beyond", and the two will disagree on real data. **The 2%-plus-volume substitution rule is the only genuinely fuzzy one**: it requires "conspicuously expanding turnover" with no published window or multiple, so it is `value: null` at the implementation level.
- **conflicts**: **This is the sharpest conflict in the canon and it is a silent one.** Both books say "3%", but they normalise differently and they scope differently. E&M's 3% is *of the stock's market price* and is applied to essentially **every** pattern boundary (H&S neckline, triangle line, rectangle line, broadening line, trendline) as a **required** confirmation, with a tighter 2% for averages. Murphy's 3% is "used **mainly** for the breaking of **longer term** trendlines", is optional ("or some variation thereof", "can be used for **added** confirmation"), carries a futures carve-out, and is offered alongside a 1% short-term alternative and a "full point" stock alternative. **A screener that implements "the 3% rule" without saying which book's version it implements has silently chosen one and mis-cited the other.** Record both.

## The channel line / Return Line

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 4 → *The Channel Line*. Edwards & Magee, Ch. 14 → *Trend Channels*.
- **definition**: A line parallel to the basic trendline, drawn across the opposite extremes. Murphy: "The channel line, or the return line as it is sometimes called". E&M: "This parallel might be called the Return Line because it marks the zone where reactions (return moves against the prevailing trend) originate. The area between Basic Trendline and Return Line is the Trend Channel."
- **criteria**:
  - construction — **basic line first, then a parallel from the first prominent peak** — "In an uptrend (see Figure 4.16a), first draw the basic up trendline along the lows Then draw a dotted line from the first prominent peak (point 2), which is parallel to the basic up trendline." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
  - confirmation — **one touch of the channel line + one return to the basic line** — "If the next rally reaches and backs off from the channel line (at point 4), then a channel may exist. If prices then drop back to the original trendline (at point 5), then a channel probably does exist." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
  - failure to reach the Return Line = deterioration — value: null — "any failure of a rally to reach the Return Line (top parallel of the channel in an Intermediate Advance) is taken as a sign of deterioration in the trend." (E&M Ch.14) — confidence(high)
  - **the symmetric-failure rule (E&M, and it is quantitative in form though unnumbered)** — value: null — "the margin by which a rally fails to reach the Return Line (before turning down) frequently equals the margin by which the Basic Trendline is penetrated by the ensuing decline before a halt or Throwback in the latter occurs." And the inverse: "when a reaction from the Return Line fails to carry prices all the way back to the Basic Trendline but bottoms out somewhere above it, the advance from that Bottom will usually push up out of the channel on the top side (through the Return Line) by a margin approximately equal to the margin by which the reaction failed to reach the bottom of the channel". (E&M Ch.14) — confidence(high) — **missing**: "frequently" / "usually" are unquantified; no sample.
  - breaking the channel line means **acceleration, not reversal** — value: null — "the breaking of a rising channel line has exactly the opposite meaning, and signals an acceleration of the existing trend." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published. Murphy: "the use of the channel line works often enough to make it worth experimenting with" — an assertion with no sample. E&M offer no rate.
- **price_objective**: The channel line itself is the objective. E&M's symmetric-failure rule above yields a *derived* objective for the subsequent penetration (the undershoot distance = the expected overshoot distance).
- **invalidation**: E&M: failure to reach the Return Line; Murphy: a break of the *basic* line ends the channel, a break of the *channel* line accelerates it.
- **detection_notes**: Computable from daily OHLCV. Primitives: fitted basic trendline (see above) + a parallel offset chosen as `max(distance from line to any pivot high)` or "the first prominent peak" — **note these two constructions give different lines and the books specify the latter**. The symmetric-failure rule needs a *signed residual* of each pivot from the channel line, retained across pivots — i.e. state, not a per-bar predicate.
- **conflicts**: Murphy treats the channel primarily as a **profit-taking / counter-trend-entry** device; E&M explicitly reject that emphasis: "Its greatest utility, however, is not what usually appeals to the beginner when he first makes its acquaintance, namely, the determination of good profit-taking levels. Experienced technicians find it more helpful in a negative sense." Record both.

## The return move / pullback / throwback

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 5 → *The Return Move*. Edwards & Magee, Ch. 6 (*the measuring formula*), Ch. 9 (*Pullbacks* bullet), Ch. 14.
- **definition**: The move back to the broken boundary after a breakout. E&M fix the vocabulary: "a Pullback or Throwback (the first is the common term for a rally after a downside breakout, and the second for a reaction following an upside breakout)".
- **criteria**:
  - E&M frequency, rectangles — **about 40%, within three days to three weeks** — "Our estimate would be that a Pullback or Throwback ... occurs within three days to three weeks in about 40% of all cases." (Ch.9) — confidence(high, as a *quote*; low, as a *measurement* — it is labelled "our estimate")
  - E&M frequency, broadening tops — **at least four out of five** — "Such a recovery (and failure) will be attempted, according to our experience, in at least four out of five Broadening Top Patterns" (Ch.10) — confidence(high as quote, low as measurement)
  - E&M depth, broadening tops — **may regain up to two-thirds; usually ≤ halfway** — "it may not fail until it has regained two-thirds of the preceding decline, although it usually peters out around or even below the halfway mark." (Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - Murphy — return move is **more common at bottoms** — value: null — "The return move is more common at bottoms than at tops and should occur on light volume." (Ch.5) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no rate published for either side.
  - Murphy — volume predicts whether it happens — value: null — "If the initial breaking of the neckline is on very heavy trading, the odds for a return move are diminished ... Lighter vol¬ ume on the initial break of the neckline increases the likelihood of a return move." (Ch.5) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no volume threshold defining "heavy" / "light".
  - the return move must NOT re-cross — value: null — "A return move will often occur back to the neck¬ line, which should not recross the neckline once it has been broken." (Murphy Ch.5, Fig 5.1a caption) — confidence(high)
  - the apex is itself the throwback target for triangles — value: null — "Note the Throwback to apex of Triangle, not an uncommon development. The apex itself is a strong Support" (E&M Ch.8, Fig 8.4 caption) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: The two E&M percentages above (40%, 4-in-5) are the only frequencies in either book, and **both are explicitly flagged by the authors as estimates**, with no sample size, universe, period, or base rate. There is no benchmark anywhere: E&M never state how often prices return to *any* arbitrary level in three weeks, so "40%" cannot be judged as skilful or trivial.
- **price_objective**: n/a.
- **invalidation**: a close back through the boundary on the wrong side. E&M: "In the case of a false move, the trend ultimately proceeds out of pattern in the opposite direction, while in the case of the premature move, the trend finally breaks out again and proceeds in the same direction."
- **detection_notes**: Computable from daily OHLCV. Primitives: breakout bar index; boundary level extended forward; a windowed test (E&M: 3 to 21 calendar days) asking whether price re-touched the boundary; a *re-cross* test on closes to distinguish a healthy throwback from a failure. The volume-predicts-throwback rule needs a breakout-day volume ratio vs a stated lookback — **neither book publishes the lookback window**, so it is `value: null`.
- **conflicts**: none material; both books describe the same phenomenon. E&M attach frequencies, Murphy does not.

## The volume signature expected during formation and at breakout

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 5 preliminaries (*Volume is More Important on the Upside*) and Ch. 6. Edwards & Magee, Ch. 6 (*Volume is important*), Ch. 7, Ch. 8 (*Volume characteristics same as the Symmetrical type*), Ch. 9.
- **definition**: Volume is treated as a required confirming variable, asymmetric between tops and bottoms.
- **criteria**:
  - the asymmetry — **upside breakouts require volume; downside breakouts do not** — "we trust, and regard as conclusive, any price break (by a decisive margin) down through the neckline of a Head-and-Shoulders Top even though it occurs on a light turnover, but we do not trust a breakout from a Head-and- Shoulders Bottom unless it is definitely attended by high volume." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
  - Murphy's version — **essential at bottoms, not critical at tops** — "At bottoms, however, the volume pick¬ up is absolutely essential. If the volume pattern does not show a significant increase during the upside price breakout, the entire price pattern should be questioned." (Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - the reason given — value: null — "It takes buying to put stocks up, but they can fall of their own weight." (E&M Ch.7, quoting a Street maxim); Murphy: "Markets have a way of 'falling of their own weight'" — confidence(high)
  - during a consolidation, volume contracts — value: null — "Volume should diminish as the price swings narrow within the triangle. This tendency for volume to contract is true of all con¬ solidation patterns." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no slope, ratio, or window published.
  - within an ascending triangle, volume tilts to rallies — value: null — "the volume tends to be slightly heavier on bounces and lighter on dips. In the descending formation, volume should be heavier on the downside and lighter during the bounces." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no ratio.
  - a broadening formation **inverts** the volume rule — value: null — "The volume tends to expand along with the wider price swings." (Murphy Ch.6); "trading activity usually remains high and irregular throughout its construction" (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - **the counter-intuitive one** — a *heavy-volume downside* break from a symmetrical triangle is evidence AGAINST the break — value: null — "The curious fact is a downside breakout from a Symmetrical Triangle attended to right from the start by conspicuously heavy volume is much more apt to be a false signal rather than the start of a genuine downtrend that will be worth following." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - rounding bottoms: the volume curve mirrors the price curve — value: null — "the tips of the volume lines at the bottom of the chart, when connected, will describe an arc that often roughly parallels the arc formed by the price" (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published anywhere in either book. There is not one volume statistic with a sample.
- **price_objective**: n/a.
- **invalidation**: absence of the expected expansion on an upside breakout disqualifies the breakout (both books). A low-volume upside breakout from a bottom is, per E&M, "only premature ... or it may be a 'false' move entirely. It pays generally to wait and see."
- **detection_notes**: **This is the single largest `value: null` cluster in the whole canon.** Every volume rule above is directional and none is numeric: not one window length, not one ratio threshold, not one significance test. To make any of them computable a screener must invent (a) a baseline window (20d? the pattern's own duration? the prior leg?), (b) an aggregation (mean, median, sum), and (c) a threshold multiple. **Whatever it picks is the screener's number, not Edwards & Magee's or Murphy's, and must be labelled that way.** The one rule that IS shape-computable without a threshold is E&M's rounding-bottom volume arc: fit a quadratic to volume over the pattern window and compare its curvature sign to the price fit — that is a genuine shape comparison, not a level test.
- **conflicts**: E&M and Murphy agree on the top/bottom asymmetry. **E&M contradict the naive reading of their own rule for symmetrical triangles** (heavy volume on a down-break = suspect), which no simple "breakout volume > X" screen can express — a volume-confirmation filter applied uniformly across patterns will actively invert E&M's triangle rule.

# PART B — REVERSAL PATTERNS

## The Head-and-Shoulders Top Formation

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 6 "Important Reversal Patterns" → *The Head-and-Shoulders Top Formation*. Murphy, Ch. 5 "Major Reversal Patterns" → *The Head and Shoulders Reversal Pattern* (pp. 103–110).
- **definition**: E&M's four-part specification, verbatim in structure: **A** "A strong rally, climaxing a more or less extensive advance, on which trading volume becomes very heavy, followed by a Minor Recession on which volume runs considerably lower ... This is the 'left shoulder.'" **B** "Another high-volume advance that reaches a higher level than the top of the left shoulder, and then another reaction on less volume that takes prices down to somewhere near the bottom level of the preceding recession ... but, in any case, below the top of the left shoulder. This is the 'Head.'" **C** "A third rally, but this time on decidedly less volume than accompanied the formation of either the left shoulder or the head, which fails to reach the height of the head before another decline sets in. This is the 'right shoulder.'" **D** "Finally, decline of prices in this third recession down through a line (the 'neckline') drawn across the Bottoms of the reactions between the left shoulder and head, and the head and right shoulder, respectively, and a close below that line by an amount approximately equivalent to 3% of the stock's market price. This is the 'confirmation' or 'breakout.'"
  Murphy's summary list: "1. A prior uptrend. 2. A left shoulder on heavier volume (point A) followed by a corrective dip to point B. 3. A rally into new highs but on lighter volume (point C). 4. A decline that moves below the previous peak (at A) and approaches the previous reaction low (point D). 5. A third rally (point E) on noticeably light volume that fails to reach the top of the head (at point C). 6. A close below the neckline. 7. A return move back to the neckline (point G) followed by new lows."
- **criteria**:
  - all four E&M elements are mandatory — value: null (boolean conjunction) — "Note that each and every item cited in A, B, C, and D of Diagram 6.1 is essential to a valid Head-and-Shoulders Top Formation. The lack of any one of them casts in doubt the forecasting value of the pattern." — confidence(high)
  - prior trend is a prerequisite — value: null — "A prerequisite for any reversal pattern is the existence of a prior trend." / "A market must obviously have something to reverse." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no minimum prior-advance magnitude or length is published by either book.
  - head must exceed both shoulders — value: null (ordinal) — "The middle peak (the head) is slightly higher than either of the two shoulders (points A and E)." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - shoulders approximately equal — value: null — "The left and right shoulders (A and E) are at about the same height." (Murphy Ch.5, Fig 5.1a caption) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no tolerance. **E&M's 3% price tolerance is stated for double/triple tops, not for shoulder equality**; borrowing it here would be an inference, not a source rule.
  - the decline from the head must break the left shoulder's top — value: null — "A decline that moves below the previous peak (at A)" (Murphy Ch.5, item 4) — confidence(high)
  - up-sloping neckline qualification — value: null — "The only qualification on an up-sloping neckline is that the Bottom of the recession between the head and right shoulder must form appreciably below the general level of the Top of the left shoulder." (E&M Ch.6) — confidence(high) — **missing**: "appreciably" is unquantified.
  - neckline slope at tops — value: null — "This line generally has a slight upward slope at tops (although it's sometimes horizontal and, less often, tilts downward)." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - confirmation margin — **3% of the stock's market price** (E&M spec D, quoted above) / **1–3% or two successive closes** (Murphy, quoted in Part A) — confidence(high)
  - right-shoulder retracement guide — **one-half to two-thirds of the C→D decline** — "(That last rally at point E will often retrace one- half to two-thirds of the decline from points C to D.)" (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - volume on head vs left shoulder — **≈1/3, 1/3, 1/3 split** — "Roughly estimated, about one-third of all confirmed Head-and-Shoulders Formations show more volume on the left shoulder than on the head, another third show about equal volume, and the final third show greater volume on the head than on the left shoulder." (E&M Ch.6) — confidence(high as quote; **this is the most important number in the chapter and it destroys the popular 'volume must decline across the peaks' screen** — see conflicts)
  - volume on the right shoulder — value: null — "The most important volume signal takes place during the third peak (the right shoulder). Volume should be noticeably lighter than on the previous two peaks." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no ratio or window.
  - abort rate before confirmation — **perhaps 20%** — "Until the neckline is broken, a certain percentage of Head-and-Shoulders developments, perhaps 20%, are 'saved'—that is, prices do not go lower, but simply flounder listlessly for a period of time in the general range of the right shoulder, then eventually firm up and renew their advance." (E&M Ch.6) — confidence(high as quote; low as measurement — "perhaps")
  - pattern is ~75% complete at the right shoulder — **75%** — "if, as they approach the approximate level of the left shoulder Top and begin to round over (volume is still relatively small), your Head-and-Shoulders Top is at least 75% completed." (E&M Ch.6) — confidence(high) — note this is a *narrative* completeness, not a measurable quantity.
- **measured_performance**: **none published.** Both books assert reliability rather than measure it. E&M: "This is one of the more common and, by all odds, the most reliable of the Major Reversal Patterns" — asserted, no sample. Murphy: "what is probably the best known and most reliable of all major reversal patterns" — asserted, no sample. E&M go further and assert rarity of false confirmations with no count: "Finally, in rare cases, a Head-and-Shoulders Top is confirmed by a decisive neckline penetration and still prices do not go down much farther. 'False moves' such as this are the most difficult phenomena with which the technical analyst has to cope. Fortunately, in the case of the Head-and-Shoulders, they are extremely rare." The **only** quasi-numbers in either treatment are E&M's "perhaps 20%" abort rate and the "one-third / one-third / one-third" volume split, and neither carries a sample size, a universe, a period, or a base rate.
- **price_objective**: **E&M (Ch.6):** "Measure the number of points down vertically from the Top of the head to the neckline as drawn in Figure 6.4. Then measure the same distance down from the neckline at the point at which prices finally penetrated it following the completion of the right shoulder. The price level thus marked is the minimum probable objective of the decline."
  **E&M's mandatory second minimum:** "we really have two minimums: one being the extent of the advance preceding the formation of the Head-and-Shoulders and the other derived from our measuring formula, whichever is the smaller will apply."
  **Murphy (Ch.5):** "Take the vertical distance from the head (point C) to the neckline. Then project that distance from the point where the neckline is broken. Assume, for example, that the top of the head is at 100 and the neckline is at 80. The vertical distance, therefore, would be the difference, which is 20. That 20 points would be mea¬ sured downward from the level at which the neckline is broken. If the neckline in Figure 5.1a is at 82 when broken, a downside objec¬ tive would be projected to the 62 level (82 - 20 = 62)."
  **Murphy's alternative:** "Another technique that accomplishes about the same task, but is a bit easier, is to simply measure the length of the first wave of the decline (points C to D) and then double it."
  **Murphy's maximum:** "The maximum objective is the size of the prior move. ... Reversal patterns can only be expected to reverse or retrace what has gone before them."
- **invalidation**: (1) failure to break the neckline by the filter — the pattern is "saved". (2) **Re-crossing after confirmation:** "Once prices have moved through the neckline and completed a head and shoulders pattern, prices should not recross the neckline again. At a top, once the neckline has been broken on the down¬ side, any decisive close back above the neckline is a serious warn¬ ing that the initial breakdown was probably a bad signal, and cre¬ ates what is often called, for obvious reasons, a failed head and shoulders." (Murphy Ch.5). (3) Missing any of E&M's A–D elements. (4) No prior trend to reverse. (5) E&M note the failure itself is informative: "A Head-and-Shoulders Formation that does not work is a warning that even though there is still some life in the situation, a genuine turn is near."
- **detection_notes**: Primitives required — pivot highs and pivot lows with explicit left/right bar counts; **five ordered pivots (H1 L1 H2 L2 H3) with H2 > max(H1,H3)**; a neckline fitted through **L1 and L2** (a 2-point line, not a horizontal — its slope is load-bearing, since E&M say a down-sloping neckline gives a later signal and Murphy says up-sloping is normal at tops); neckline extrapolation forward to the breakout bar; a percentage-penetration test on closes; a rolling volume aggregate at each of the three peaks; a forward window for the return-move test.
  **Explicitly NOT computable from daily OHLCV alone / requires shape comparison, not pivot detection:**
  - **"shoulders at about the same height"** has no published tolerance. This is a *symmetry* comparison between two pivots, and you must supply the tolerance yourself.
  - **E&M's "strong tendency toward symmetry"** (Ch.6/7) is a comparison of the *left half's shape* to the *right half's shape* — duration, slope, and pivot spacing — not a level test. Multiple/complex variants make this mandatory rather than optional.
  - **"the right shoulder ... fails to reach the height of the head"** is a level test and IS computable; **"the right shoulder resembles the left in price confirmation"** is not.
  - E&M's parallel-line right-shoulder guide ("a line parallel to the neckline, extending from the top of the left shoulder through the head and on to the right") is computable and is a genuinely useful anticipatory primitive.
  - Volume ratios need a window the books never publish (see Part A).
  - E&M's "**75% completed**" is not a measurable quantity at all — it is a rhetorical statement about how much of the pattern's *narrative* has elapsed. Do not attempt to compute it.
- **conflicts**:
  - **On volume across the three peaks — E&M and Murphy directly disagree, and this is the highest-value finding in this file.** Murphy states a declining-volume expectation as a "strong tendency": "As a general rule, the second peak (the head) should take place on lighter volume than the left shoulder. This is not a requirement, but a strong tendency and an early warning of diminishing buying pressure." **Edwards & Magee report the opposite as roughly a coin-flip**: "about one-third of all confirmed Head-and-Shoulders Formations show more volume on the left shoulder than on the head, another third show about equal volume, and the final third show greater volume on the head than on the left shoulder." A screener that *requires* head-volume < left-shoulder-volume implements Murphy and rejects two-thirds of E&M's confirmed population. Record both; do not merge.
  - **On the measured move — E&M impose a cap that Murphy does not.** E&M make the *smaller* of (pattern height, prior advance) the operative minimum. Murphy states the pattern-height projection as the minimum and treats the prior move only as a separate *maximum*. These are different formulas and give different targets whenever the prior advance is small.
  - **Against a third authority (Bulkowski, thepatternsite.com — secondary):** where E&M and Murphy assert "most reliable", Bulkowski publishes a measured **"19%" break-even failure rate**, **"16%"** average decline, **"51%"** meeting the price target, and a **"68%"** pullback rate, on a base of "more than 2,800 perfect trades", updated 8/26/2020. The "51% meeting the price target" figure is a direct, sampled refutation of the confident tone of E&M's Ch.11 remark that "it is amazing how accurately that formula works out". Note Bulkowski publishes no base rate either (no benchmark for what a random 16% decline rate would be over the same universe and period), so his numbers are measured but still un-benchmarked.

## Head-and-Shoulders (EN: or Kilroy) Bottoms / The Inverse Head and Shoulders

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 7 "Important Reversal Patterns: continued" → *Head-and-Shoulders (EN: or Kilroy) Bottoms*. Murphy, Ch. 5 → *The Inverse Head and Shoulders* (p. 110). **The "Kilroy Bottom" name is Bassetti's 11th-edition coinage, not Edwards & Magee's** — "The present Editor has always been impatient with the undescriptive nature of the term 'Head-and-Shoulders Bottom,' and so he has renamed it the 'Kilroy Bottom.'"
- **definition**: E&M's mirrored A–D spec (portions differing from the top are in the book's italics): **A** "A decline, climaxing a more or less extensive downtrend, on which trading volume increases notably, followed by a Minor Recovery, on which volume runs less than it did during the days of final decline and at the Bottom. This is the 'left shoulder.'" **B** "Another decline that carries prices below the Bottom of the left shoulder, on which activity shows some increase ... followed by another recovery that carries above the Bottom level of the left shoulder and on which activity may pick up, or at any rate exceed that on the recovery from the left shoulder. This is the 'head.'" **C** "A third decline on decidedly less volume ... which fails to reach the low level of the head before another rally starts. This is the 'right shoulder.'" **D** "Finally, an advance on which activity increases notably, which pushes up through the neckline ... and closes above by an amount approximately equivalent to 3% of the stock's market price, with a conspicuous burst of activity attending this penetration. This is the 'confirmation' or 'breakout.'"
- **criteria**:
  - the 3% margin — **3% of the stock's market price**, on a close (E&M spec D, quoted above) — confidence(high)
  - **volume is NOT optional here (this is the load-bearing difference from the top)** — value: null — "It must be present on the penetration of the neckline, or else the breakout is not to be relied on as a decisive confirmation." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no threshold defines "present"/"conspicuous burst".
  - Murphy's version of the same — value: null — "The critical point occurs at the rally through the neckline. This signal must be accompanied by a sharp burst of trading volume if the breakout is for real. ... At the bottom, heavy volume is an absolutely essential ingredient in the completion of the basing pattern." (Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - volume shape in the second half differs from the top — value: null — "The rally from the head, however, should begin to show not only an increase in trading activity, but the level of volume often exceeds that registered on the rally from the left shoulder." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - bottoms are longer and flatter than tops — value: null — "It can be said that Bottoms are generally longer and flatter, that is, they take more time in relation to depth of pattern in points than do Tops." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no duration or aspect-ratio number.
  - neckline slope at bottoms — value: null — "For basing patterns, most necklines have a slight downward tilt." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - return move more common at bottoms — value: null — "One slight difference at the bottom is the greater ten¬ dency for the return move back to the neckline to occur after the bullish breakout." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high) — but note **E&M say the opposite in frequency terms**, see conflicts.
- **measured_performance**: none published. Both books assert the pattern's reliability; neither reports a sample.
- **price_objective**: Identical to the top, mirrored. E&M: "The measuring implications of the Head-and-Shoulders Bottom are the same in all respects and are applied in the same way as with Tops." Murphy: "A decisive close through the neckline is also nec¬ essary to complete the pattern, and the measuring technique is the same." E&M's two-minimums cap (smaller of pattern height and the prior decline) carries over.
- **invalidation**: a low-volume neckline break is not a confirmation (E&M, above) — this is a *stronger* invalidation than exists at the top. E&M: "A low-volume breakout from a Bottom Pattern may only be premature ... or it may be a 'false' move entirely. It pays generally to wait and see." Re-crossing the neckline after confirmation invalidates (Murphy's failed-H&S rule, symmetric).
- **detection_notes**: Same primitives as the top, mirrored (five ordered pivots L1 H1 L2 H2 L3 with L2 < min(L1,L3), neckline through H1/H2). **The one asymmetry that MUST be encoded: the volume gate is mandatory on the upside break and optional on the downside break.** A single symmetric `breakout_volume_ratio >= X` rule applied to both tops and bottoms is not what either book says. Same un-computable items as the top: shoulder-equality tolerance, left/right shape symmetry, "conspicuous burst". E&M's "bottoms are longer and flatter" is a shape claim comparing duration to depth — computable only if you fix your own aspect normalisation.
- **conflicts**:
  - **On return-move frequency, Murphy and E&M disagree.** Murphy: the return move is "more common at bottoms than at tops". E&M: "Reactions to the neckline following the initial breakout from the Bottom type appear in **about the same frequency and proportions** as do the Pullback Rallies, which follow the initial breakdown from the Top type." Record both.
  - **Nomenclature:** "inverse head and shoulders" (Murphy) vs "Head-and-Shoulders Bottom" (Edwards & Magee 1948) vs "Kilroy Bottom" (Bassetti, 11th ed. only). A pattern-name registry should carry all three with the correct attribution; the third is an editor's coinage and should not be attributed to Edwards & Magee.

## Multiple Head-and-Shoulders Patterns / Complex Head and Shoulders

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 7 → *Multiple Head-and-Shoulders Patterns* and *Tendency to symmetry*. Murphy, Ch. 5 → *Complex Head and Shoulders Patterns* (p. 113).
- **definition**: E&M: "Head-and-Shoulders Reversals in which either the shoulders or the head, or both, has been doubled or proliferated into several distinct waves." Murphy: "These are patterns where two heads may appear or a double left and right shoulder."
- **criteria**:
  - symmetry is strong enough to trade on — value: null — "Patterns of the Multiple or Complex type show an even stronger urge toward symmetry—so strong, in fact, that it may be counted on in determining trading policy. If there are two shoulders on the left, there are almost always two on the right of nearly the same size and duration. ... Except in volume, the right-hand half of the pattern is, in the great majority of cases, an approximate mirror image of the left." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high) — **missing**: "nearly the same size and duration" has no tolerance; "the great majority" has no number.
  - Murphy's version — value: null — "there is the strong tendency toward symmetry in the head and shoulders pattern. This means that a single left shoulder usually indicates a single right shoulder. A double left shoulder increases the odds of a dou¬ ble right shoulder." (Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - necklines are near-horizontal in complex forms — value: null — "Up-sloping and down-sloping variants seldom appear in this class of patterns; necklines are almost always very close to the horizontal." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
  - **the inner/outer neckline rule** — value: null — "More often, there are two necklines, an inner and an outer, and no price movement of consequence is to be expected until the outer has been penetrated" (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
  - frequency — value: null — "Formations of this type appear with fair frequency at Primary Bottoms and Tops, but more often at Bottoms than at Tops. They appear less frequently at Intermediate Reversals." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high); Murphy says "These patterns are not that common" — confidence(high). **See conflicts.**
- **measured_performance**: none published by either book.
- **price_objective**: Both books say the implications are the same as the simple form. Murphy: "have the same forecasting implications." E&M give no separate formula; the head-to-neckline projection applies, but with the **outer** neckline as the reference line per the rule above.
- **invalidation**: penetration of the inner neckline alone is not a signal (E&M). Otherwise as for the simple form.
- **detection_notes**: **This pattern cannot be found by pivot-count matching and is the clearest case in the canon where SHAPE COMPARISON is mandatory rather than optional.** Primitives: a variable-length ordered pivot sequence; a *left-half / right-half segmentation* around the head (or heads); and a similarity score comparing the two halves on **pivot count, pivot spacing in bars, and swing amplitude** — E&M's own decision rule ("two shoulders on the left ⇒ almost always two on the right of nearly the same size and duration") is literally a half-vs-half shape match. A screener built only on "find 5 pivots" will never see this pattern. Additionally the inner/outer neckline rule requires fitting **two** candidate lines through different subsets of the intervening lows and selecting the outer envelope. All of this is computable from daily OHLCV, but none of it is computable from a fixed-arity pivot template.
- **conflicts**: **On frequency, E&M and Murphy disagree.** E&M: "Formations of this type appear with fair frequency ... Still another form, of which you will usually find several good examples at any Major Market Turn". Murphy: "These patterns are not that common". Record both.

## The Continuation Head and Shoulders Pattern / Head-and-Shoulders Consolidations

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 6 "Continuation Patterns" → *The Continuation Head and Shoulders Pattern* (p. 153). Edwards & Magee, Ch. 11 "Consolidation Formations" → *Head-and-Shoulders Consolidations*.
- **definition**: Murphy: "In the continuation head and shoulders variety, prices trace out a pattern that looks very similar to a sideways rectangu¬ lar pattern except that the middle trough in an uptrend (see Figure 6.11a) tends to be lower than either of the two shoulders. In a downtrend (see Figure 6.11b), the middle peak in the consol¬ idation exceeds the other two peaks. The result in both cases is a head and shoulders pattern turned upside down. Because it is turned upside down, there is no chance of confusing it with the reversal pattern."
- **criteria**:
  - orientation is the whole discriminator — value: null (boolean) — an *inverse* H&S occurring inside an **uptrend** is a continuation; an ordinary H&S top shape occurring inside a **downtrend** is a continuation (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - **volume inverts** — value: null — "The volume pattern in Consolidations of this type does not follow the rule for Reversal Head and Shoulders. In a downtrend, for example, the Consolidation Formation resembled in its price contour a Head-and-Shoulders Top, but the attendant volume will diminish instead of increase on the left shoulder and head as well as on the right shoulder." (E&M Ch.11) — confidence(high)
  - breakouts behave normally — value: null — "however, Breakouts resemble, in all respects, those arising from Reversal Formations." (E&M Ch.11) — confidence(high)
  - complex continuation variants are rare — value: null — "Head-and-Shoulders Consolidations of the Complex or Multiple type seldom appear on the charts." (E&M Ch.11) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published. Murphy: "on occasion, act as a consolidation rather than a reversal pattern. When this does happen, it's the exception rather than the rule" — asserted frequency, no number, no sample.
- **price_objective**: **E&M explicitly warn the standard formula degrades here**: "the same formula applied to Consolidation Patterns of the Head-and-Shoulders form does not work out as well. Such patterns are usually quite 'flat,' and the ensuing move generally" [carries further than the flat pattern implies — the sentence continues across an OCR page break; treat the *direction* of the caveat as sourced and the completion as unverified]. Murphy gives no separate formula.
- **invalidation**: as for the reversal form.
- **detection_notes**: Computable, and cheap: it is the *reversal* detector plus a **prior-trend sign test**. The critical implementation note is that the trend context, not the shape, decides the label — the same five pivots mean opposite things depending on the sign of the preceding move. **A pattern library that classifies on shape alone will mislabel every one of these.** The volume inversion means a "volume must expand into the head" gate would reject exactly the continuation cases.
- **conflicts**: none between the two books; both treat it as a real but secondary case. **Internal conflict within E&M**: the H&S measuring formula is asserted to work "amazingly" for reversals and explicitly not as well for consolidations, so a single measured-move implementation across both is contrary to the source.

## Double Tops and Bottoms

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 9 "More important Reversal Patterns" → *Double and Triple Tops and Bottoms*, *Distinguishing characteristics*, *Double Bottoms*. Murphy, Ch. 5 → *Double Tops and Bottoms* (p. 117) and *Variations from the Ideal Pattern* (p. 121).
- **definition**: E&M: "A Double Top is formed when a stock advances to a certain level with, usually, high volume at and approaching the Top figure, then retreats with diminishing activity, then comes up again to the same (or practically the same) top price as before with some pickup in turnover, but not as much as on the first peak, and then finally turns down a second time for a Major or Consequential Intermediate Decline." Murphy: "the top is often referred to as an 'M' and the bottom as a 'W.'"
- **criteria**:
  - **confirmation is the break of the intervening trough, on a close** — value: null (level test) — "the reversal is not complete until the previous support point at B is violated on a dosing basis." (Murphy Ch.5; "dosing" is an OCR error for "closing") — confidence(high)
  - E&M's version — value: null — "If prices, on their recession from the second peak, drop through the Bottom level of the valley, a Reversal of Trend from up to down is signaled" — confidence(high)
  - **minimum separation between the two peaks — E&M: more than a month** — "if the two Tops are more than a month apart, they are not likely to belong to the same Consolidation or Congestion Formation." — confidence(high)
  - **minimum separation — Murphy: at least a month** — "In general, most valid double tops or bottoms should have at least a month between the two peaks or troughs. Some will even be two or three months apart." — confidence(high)
  - **minimum depth of the intervening reaction — 20% of the top value** — "If, in addition, the reaction between the first and second high reduces prices by 20% of their top value, the odds swing toward a Double Top" (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high) — **this is the single most specific, most computable, and most widely ignored E&M criterion in the whole canon.**
  - **tolerance for "same level" between the two peaks — 3%** — "Also, the three highs may not come at precisely the same price; our 3% tolerance rule is again useful here." (E&M Ch.9, stated for triple tops and applied to double tops immediately above: "A first Top at 50, for example, and a second at 51 1/2 would come within this limit.") — confidence(high)
  - the second peak commonly exceeds the first slightly — value: null — "Curiously enough, the second peak often does exceed the first by a fraction." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high); Murphy concurs: "What does cause some problems is when the second peak actually exceeds the first peak by a slight margin."
  - the two operative conditions (E&M) — value: null — "The important points are (1) that buying cannot push prices up into the clear by a decisive margin, and (2) the Support below is subsequently broken." — confidence(high)
  - volume — value: null — "Volume tends to be heavier during the first peak and lighter on the second." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no ratio.
  - the second bottom of a double bottom is dull and rounded — value: null — "the second Bottom is usually conspicuously dull (little trading volume) and is apt to be quite rounded, whereas the second Top in a Double Top is moderately active and nearly as sharp and 'spiky' in contour as the first." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high) — **this is an explicit SHAPE asymmetry between the top and bottom versions.**
  - rarity — value: null — "True Double Tops and Double Bottoms are exceedingly rare; Triple Forms are even rarer." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published. Both books are unusually blunt that the *name* is over-applied. Murphy has a section headed "The Term 'Double Top' Greatly Overused": "Most potential double tops or bottoms wind up being something else. The reason for this is that prices have a strong tendency to back off from a previous peak or bounce off a previous low. These price changes are a natural reaction and do not in themselves constitute a reversal pattern." E&M: "It is referred to by name perhaps more often than any other chart pattern by traders who possess a smattering of technical 'lingo' but little organized knowledge of technical facts." **Neither supplies a false-positive rate.**
- **price_objective**: **Murphy (Ch.5):** "The measuring technique for the double top is the height of the pattern projected from the breakdown point (the point where the middle trough at point B is broken). As an alternative, measure the height of the first downleg (points A to B) and project that length downward from the middle trough at point B."
  **Edwards & Magee (Ch.9) — and note they explicitly downgrade it:** "As to measuring implications, the Double Top affords no formula comparable with what we have attributed to Head-and-Shoulders and Triangle Formations, but it is safe to assume the decline will continue at least as far below the valley level as the distance from peak to valley."
  E&M's universal cap applies: "(Take into account the general rule that a Reversal Formation can be expected to produce no more than a retracement of the trend that preceded it.)"
- **invalidation**: The pattern does not exist until the valley is broken — this is stated forcefully by both. Murphy: "Remember that, at a top, prices must actually violate the previous reaction low before the double top exists." Until then the odds favour continuation: "the odds usually favor continuation of the present trend" (Murphy Ch.5, on a mere pullback from a prior peak). A second peak that clears the first *decisively* (beyond the 3% tolerance and the filters) is a resumption, not a double top — though Murphy warns it can still be a "bull trap".
- **detection_notes**: Highly computable — this is the best-specified pattern in the canon for a screener. Primitives: two pivot highs P1, P2; one intervening pivot low V; tests: `|P2 - P1| / P1 <= 0.03` (E&M's tolerance); `(P1 - V) / P1 >= 0.20` (E&M's depth rule); `bars_between(P1, P2) > ~21 trading days` (E&M "more than a month" / Murphy "at least a month"); confirmation `close < V` with the chosen penetration filter. Volume comparison at P1 vs P2 needs a window the books do not publish.
  **The one genuinely shape-dependent criterion**: E&M's claim that the second *bottom* is "quite rounded" while the second *top* is "sharp and spiky". Computing "rounded vs spiky" requires a curvature or dwell-time measure around the pivot (e.g. how many bars sit within N% of the extreme), not a pivot test. This is computable from daily OHLCV but is a *shape* primitive, and it is the discriminator E&M give for distinguishing a real double bottom from a bounce.
- **conflicts**:
  - **On the measured move, E&M and Murphy disagree in confidence, not in arithmetic.** Murphy states the height projection as *the* measuring technique with an alternative. E&M say the double top "affords no formula comparable" and then offer the same arithmetic as a safe assumption. Same number, opposite epistemic status. Record both.
  - **On separation, E&M and Murphy agree ("more than a month" / "at least a month") — this is one of the few places the two books converge on a number**, which strengthens it.
  - **The 20% depth rule is E&M's alone.** Murphy publishes no depth requirement at all. A screener that omits it is implementing Murphy; one that includes it is implementing E&M. Do not present it as "the classical rule" without attribution.

## Triple Tops and Bottoms

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 9 → *Triple Tops and Bottoms*. Murphy, Ch. 5 → *Triple Tops and Bottoms* (p. 115).
- **definition**: Murphy: "The main difference is that the three peaks or troughs in the triple top or bottom are at about the same level." E&M: "The true Triple Top (as distinct, that is, from other types of three-peak formations) carries a recognizable family resemblance to the Double Top. Its Tops are widely spaced and with quite depth and usually rounding reactions between them."
- **criteria**:
  - three peaks at about the same level — **3% tolerance (E&M)** — "Also, the three highs may not come at precisely the same price; our 3% tolerance rule is again useful here." — confidence(high)
  - **completion requires BOTH intervening troughs to break (Murphy)** — value: null — "The triple top is not complete until support levels along both of the inter¬ vening lows have been broken. Conversely, prices must close through the two intervening peaks at the bottom to complete a triple bottom." — confidence(high)
  - **Murphy's alternate, weaker trigger** — value: null — "(As an alternate strategy, the breaking of the nearest peak or trough can also be used as a reversal signal.)" — confidence(high)
  - **E&M require only the LOWER valley to break** — value: null — "The conclusive test, however, is a decline from the third Top that breaks prices down through the level of the valley floor (the lower one, if the two valleys form at different levels)." — confidence(high) — **see conflicts, this is a real disagreement.**
  - spacing need not be uniform — **illustrative: 2nd ≈3 weeks after 1st, 3rd ≥6 weeks after 2nd** — "Thus, the second Top may occur only about three weeks after the first and the third six weeks or more after the second." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(med) — this is an *example* of permissible irregularity, not a rule; do not encode as a threshold.
  - the valleys need not be level — value: null — "Also, the intervening valleys need not bottom out at exactly the same level; the first may be shallower than the second and vice versa." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high)
  - volume declines across the three peaks — value: null — "Volume is characteristically less on the second advance than on the first, and still less on the third, which often peters out with no appreciable pickup in activity." (E&M Ch.9); Murphy: "The volume tends to decline with each successive peak at the top and should increase at the breakdown point." — confidence(high) — **missing**: no ratio or window. **Note this is a monotonic-decline requirement — and it is exactly the requirement E&M refused to impose on the H&S. The two patterns are treated inconsistently within the same book.**
  - rarity — value: null — "The fact is that Reversal Formations, which can only be classed as Triple Tops, do occur, but they are few and far between." (E&M Ch.9); Murphy: "which is much rarer in occurrence" — confidence(high)
  - it may be a rectangle instead — value: null — "Many patterns evolve at an important turn from up to down in the trend that contains three Top points, but most fall more readily into the category of Rectangles." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published. E&M and Murphy both classify it as a rare variant. Murphy: "Chartists often disagree as to whether a reversal pattern is a head and shoulders or a triple top. The argument is academic, because both pat¬ terns imply the exact same thing."
- **price_objective**: Murphy: "The measuring implication is also similar to the head and shoulders, and is based on the height of the pattern. Prices will usually move a minimum distance from the breakout point at least equal to the height of the pattern." Figure 5.4a caption: "The measuring technique is the height of the pattern projected downward from the breakdown point." E&M give no separate formula for the triple form.
- **invalidation**: prices holding above the valley floor(s). E&M: "so long as demand persists at the valley price range, the trend can be turned up again."
- **detection_notes**: Computable. Primitives: three pivot highs within a 3% band; two intervening pivot lows; **two different completion tests depending on which book you implement** (both troughs vs the lower trough). Requires a monotonic volume comparison across three windows — window unpublished. The E&M/Murphy note that a triple top is often better classified as a rectangle or an H&S means a pattern library needs an explicit **precedence rule** between these three labels or it will emit duplicate, contradictory hits on the same bars. Neither book supplies that precedence rule.
- **conflicts**: **Murphy requires both intervening lows to break; Edwards & Magee require only the lower one.** On a triple top with unequal valleys these give different signal dates and different measured-move reference points. Record both; this is not a paraphrase difference.

## Rounding Tops and Bottoms (Saucer / Bowl / Inverted Bowl) and the Dormant Bottom

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 7 → *Rounding Tops and Bottoms*, *How Rounding Turns affect trading activity*, *The Dormant Bottom variation*, *Volume pattern at Tops*. Murphy, Ch. 5 → *Saucers and Spikes* (p. 125).
- **definition**: E&M: "Rounding Bottoms are commonly referred to as Bowl or Saucer Patterns. Rounding Tops are sometimes called Inverted Bowls." Murphy: "The saucer bottom shows a very slow and very gradual turn from down to sideways to up."
- **criteria**:
  - where they occur — value: null — "Rounding Bottoms occur most often in low-priced stocks, in an extended, flat-bottomed form that usually takes many months to complete." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
  - timeframe — value: null — "Saucer bottoms are usually spotted on weekly or monthly charts that span several years. The longer they last, the more significant they become." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no minimum duration.
  - **the volume signature is the pattern's own arc** — value: null — "In such formations, the tips of the volume lines at the bottom of the chart, when connected, will describe an arc that often roughly parallels the arc formed by the price" (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
  - volume sequence at the bottom — value: null — "Volume, which has been running high, gradually decreases. ... At the Bottom, with the two forces technically in balance, relatively few transactions are recorded. Then demand begins to increase, and as the price curve turns up, trading becomes more active. Volume accelerates with the trend until often it reaches a sort of climactic peak in a few days of almost 'vertical' price movement on the chart." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
  - **volume at rounding TOPS is NOT clean** — value: null — "The volume pattern on Rounding Tops is seldom as clearly defined as at Bottoms. Indeed, it is apt to be rather high and irregular throughout the entire rounding-over movement in prices." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high)
  - premature breakout is normal — value: null — "The jump out of line on June 10 and subsequent return to the Saucer Pattern is a common development in Rounding Bottoms." (E&M Ch.7, Fig 7.20 caption) — confidence(high)
  - **Dormant Bottom** — value: null — "It appears characteristically in 'thin' stocks ... a normal day's turnover may be only two or three hundred shares in an active rising market. Finally, weeks and sometimes months will pass during which no sales will be registered for days at a time, or only an occasional lot at a figure that fluctuates within a fractional range, making the chart appear 'flyspecked.'" then "a sudden and usually quite inexplicable flurry of activity. Several hundred shares appear on the tape and prices advance sharply." (E&M Ch.7) — confidence(high) — **missing**: "two or three hundred shares" is a 1948 illustration, not a threshold.
- **measured_performance**: none published. E&M assert without measuring: "they almost never deceive." That is the strongest reliability claim in the book and it carries **no sample, no count, and no base rate.**
- **price_objective**: **NONE. Both books explicitly decline to give one — this is the notable case.**
  E&M (Ch.7): "**We know of no measuring formula that can be applied to Rounding Turns** (except for the minimum qualifications we mentioned in connection with Head-and-Shoulders, that is, they cannot be counted on to produce a greater move than the preceding price swing in the opposite direction), but they almost never deceive. Their implications can be roughly estimated from the magnitude of the trends that led to them and the length of time they take in the rounding-over process."
  Murphy (Ch.5): "**It is difficult to tell exactly when the saucer has been completed or to measure how far prices will travel in the opposite direction.**"
  So: `price_objective: null`, with only the universal prior-swing cap surviving.
- **invalidation**: there is no defined breakout level, hence no defined failure. E&M's only structural caveat is that a premature jump out of the saucer and return is normal and does not invalidate.
- **detection_notes**: **This pattern is not a pivot pattern and cannot be found by pivot detection.** Primitives: (1) a quadratic (or spline) fit to lows over a rolling window, with a **positive curvature** requirement and a low residual; (2) the same fit applied to volume, with a **negative** curvature requirement (bowl-shaped price, dome-shaped volume is wrong — E&M say the volume arc *parallels* the price arc, i.e. also bowl-shaped for a bottom); (3) **weekly or monthly resampling is effectively mandatory** — Murphy says these are "usually spotted on weekly or monthly charts that span several years", so a daily-bar-only screener will systematically miss them; (4) a completeness/lip test comparing the right lip to the left lip. **The absence of a completion trigger is the real engineering problem: without a breakout level there is no event date, so a saucer cannot be scored the way a neckline break can.** The Dormant Bottom additionally needs zero-volume / no-trade day detection and a fractional-range test — computable, but on modern consolidated tape "no sales for days at a time" is essentially extinct outside micro-caps, so this variant should be expected to fire almost never.
- **conflicts**: **Edwards & Magee and Murphy agree there is no measuring formula. A third named authority disagrees and publishes one.** Bulkowski (secondary, thepatternsite.com/roundb.html) gives an explicit measure rule — "Compute the height from the left saucer lip to the lowest valley in the pattern then multiply it by the above 'percentage meeting price target.' Add the difference to the right saucer lip to get a price target." — together with a measured **"4%" break-even failure rate, "48%" average rise, "65%" meeting price target, "64%" throwback rate, on "990 perfect trades"**. **If a screener publishes a rounding-bottom target, it is implementing Bulkowski, not the classical canon, and must say so.** Note also that Bulkowski's 4% failure rate is the closest any measured source comes to vindicating E&M's un-sampled "they almost never deceive" — but it is a different claim over a different, undisclosed universe with no base rate, so it corroborates the sentiment, not the assertion.

## Scallops: repeated Saucers

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 11 "Consolidation Formations" → *Scallops: repeated Saucers*. **Murphy does not cover this pattern.**
- **definition**: "When a stock for which there is a large number of shares outstanding, and for which there is, at all times, a fairly active and 'close' market emerges from a long-time Bottom ..., which will often make a long Major Advance in a series of 'Saucers.' These successive patterns, each of which resembles, in both price and volume action, the Reversal Formation described in Chapter 7 as a Rounding Bottom, are slightly uptilted, that is, the rising end always carries the price a little higher than the preceding Top at the beginning of the Saucer."
- **criteria**:
  - each unit is a rounding bottom in price AND volume — value: null — quoted above — confidence(high)
  - **each saucer's right lip exceeds the previous saucer's left lip** — value: null — "the rising end always carries the price a little higher than the preceding Top at the beginning of the Saucer" — confidence(high)
  - no boundary lines exist — value: null — "it does not constitute a more or less definite area of Congestion or fluctuation to which one or more critical boundary lines can be affixed." — confidence(high)
  - universe restriction — value: null — "a stock for which there is a large number of shares outstanding, and for which there is, at all times, a fairly active and 'close' market" — confidence(high) — **missing**: no float or liquidity threshold.
  - gain per scallop varies — value: null — "The net gain accomplished by each Saucering movement will vary from stock to stock" — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: none given in the text read.
- **invalidation**: a saucer whose right lip fails to exceed the prior left lip breaks the sequence.
- **detection_notes**: Requires the rounding-bottom shape primitive (curvature fit) applied **repeatedly and chained**, plus an inter-pattern level comparison. This is a *sequence-of-shapes* detector, not a pattern detector, and is the most structurally demanding item in this file. Computable from daily OHLCV in principle; needs weekly resampling in practice. E&M's universe restriction (large float, continuously liquid) is a real screen filter and is one of the few places the book tells you *what not to scan*.
- **conflicts**: Murphy omits the pattern entirely — a coverage gap, not a disagreement.

## Saucers and Spikes / V-formations (and the One-Day Reversal, Selling Climax, Spikes, Runaway Days)

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 5 → *Saucers and Spikes* (p. 125). Edwards & Magee, Ch. 10 "Other Reversal phenomena" → *The One-Day Reversals*, *The Selling Climax*, *Short-term phenomena of potential importance*, *Spikes*, *Runaway Days*, *Key Reversal Days*. **The "Spikes / Runaway Days / Key Reversal Days" subsections are Bassetti's additions in the later editions, not the 1948 Edwards & Magee text.**
- **definition**: Murphy: "the spike (or V pattern) happens very quickly with little or no transi¬ tion period. They usually take place in a market that has gotten so overextended in one direction, that a sudden piece of adverse news causes the market to reverse direction very abruptly." Bassetti: "On the day it occurs, a Spike is not immediately identifiable for by definition it protrudes Head-and-Shoulders above days before and after if it is at or near a Top and plunges much below the surrounding days if it occurs at a Bottom."
- **criteria**:
  - it is only visible in hindsight — value: null — "So after a day that exhibits an unusually wide range, the subsequent days must be observed to discriminate the day from a Runaway Day." (Bassetti, Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - the three factors that grade a spike — value: null — "1. The strength and length of the action that preceded it. 2. The close of the day, whether up on a Bottom or down on a Top. 3. Its prominence when compared with the days before and after it." (Bassetti, Ch.10) — confidence(high) — **missing**: "prominence" has no metric; "unusually wide range" has no multiple.
  - the only advance warning is a heavy-volume reversal bar — value: null — "A daily or weekly reversal, on very heavy volume, is sometimes the only warning they give us." (Murphy Ch.5) — confidence(high)
  - **E&M rate the one-day reversal as a weak major signal** — value: null — "Remember, a One-Day Reversal is not a dependable Major Trend indicator." — confidence(high)
  - E&M's historical count for a general-market one-day reversal marking a primary low — **exactly one occasion (April 1939)** — "on only one occasion (April 1939) in history has a general market One-Day Reversal signaled the final low of a Primary Bear Trend" and "Only one Primary Downtrend in all the record has, in fact, ended with the first Panic Phase, that being the five-month Bear Market of 1938-1939" — confidence(high as quote; the "record" is unspecified and ends in the 1940s)
  - weekly reversals mostly disappoint — value: null — "most 'Reversal weeks' are followed by very disappointing moves." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - a weekly reversal (Murphy's construction) — value: null — "An upside weekly reversal, therefore, would occur when the market trades lower during the week, makes a new low for the move, but on Friday closes above the previous Friday's close." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published, and both books say so in effect. Murphy: "there's not much more we can say about them except that we hope you don't run into too many of them." E&M's "one occasion in history" is a count of one over an unstated denominator — it is an anecdote, not a rate.
- **price_objective**: **none given by either book for the V/spike.** Murphy explicitly gives no measuring rule. `price_objective: null`.
- **invalidation**: undefined — there is no entry level and therefore no defined failure.
- **detection_notes**: **Borderline out of scope for this file** — the spike/one-day reversal/key reversal day are *single-bar* phenomena, which the brief excludes, and only the *V-formation* (the multi-bar shape around them) is in scope. The multi-bar V is computable but with no published criteria: you would need (1) a slope test on the N bars before and after the extreme, (2) a symmetry test comparing the two slopes, (3) a "little or no transition period" test = a maximum bar count at the extreme. **Every threshold there is yours, not the books'.** The one genuinely sourced computable is Murphy's weekly reversal: resample to weekly, test `low[w] < min(low[w-k..w-1])` and `close[w] > close[w-1]` — this **requires weekly resampling** and is not expressible on daily bars without it.
- **conflicts**: Murphy groups saucers and spikes together as the two "hard" reversal patterns; E&M scatter the material across one-day reversals, selling climaxes, and Bassetti's later spike/runaway-day additions. The 11th-edition additions are **editor's material** and should not be attributed to Edwards & Magee.

## The Broadening Formations (and the Orthodox Broadening Top, Right-Angled Broadening Formations)

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 10 → *The Broadening Formations*, *Volume during Broadening Formations*, *The Orthodox Broadening Top*, *Right-Angled Broadening Formations*. Murphy, Ch. 6 → *The Broadening Formation* (p. 140).
- **definition**: E&M: "starting with very narrow fluctuations, they widen out between diverging rather than converging boundary lines." Murphy: "the trendlines actually diverge in the broadening forma¬ tion, creating a picture that looks like an expanding triangle. It is also called a megaphone top." E&M reject the "inverted triangle" name: "But their causes, characteristics, and forecasting implications are so radically different that we have chosen to rename them Broadening Formations".
  **Orthodox Broadening Top (E&M's precise special case):** "The Orthodox Broadening Top has three peaks at successively higher levels with two Bottoms between them, with the second Bottom lower than the first. The assumption has been it is completed and in effect as an important Reversal indication just as soon as the reaction from its third peak carries below the level of its second Bottom."
- **criteria**:
  - **structure — 3 rising peaks, 2 falling troughs** — "three peaks at successively higher levels with two Bottoms between them, with the second Bottom lower than the first" (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high). Murphy's Fig 6.5 caption agrees: "It shows three successively higher peaks and two declin¬ ing troughs. The violation of the second trough completes the pattern."
  - completion — **break below the second (lower) trough** — quoted above, both books — confidence(high)
  - **volume INVERTS the normal consolidation rule** — value: null — "The volume tends to expand along with the wider price swings." (Murphy Ch.6); "trading activity usually remains high and irregular throughout its construction" (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - **bearish bias — E&M: 9 times out of 10** — "we have already noted 9 times out of 10 they carry Bearish implications. They appear most often at or near an important topping out of the trend." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high as quote; **asserted, no sample, no period, no universe, no base rate**)
  - E&M's basis for that claim — **~20 years of chart-watching, unmeasured** — "Hence, after studying the charts for some 20 years and watching what market action has followed the appearance of Broadening Price Patterns, we have come to the conclusion they are definitely Bearish in purport" — confidence(high)
  - **the "failure" early-warning rule** — value: null — "If the trend is going to break down from the Broadening Area, the last rally within the area may fail to rise as high as its predecessor, thus breaking the sequence of ever higher Tops within the pattern. Alternatively, if the trend is going to emerge on the topside, the last reaction within the pattern may fail to depress prices as low as the preceding reaction. These 'failures' within the pattern occur ... in a majority of all Broadening Formations." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high) — **missing**: "a majority" has no number.
  - the failure is only confirmed by a 3% move — **3%** — "one cannot be sure of such a significant development ... until prices go on and out the other side of the formation or, more precisely, have exceeded the last preceding move in that direction by a decisive margin (our 3% rule again)." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - pullback frequency after the breakdown — **at least four out of five** — "Such a recovery (and failure) will be attempted, according to our experience, in at least four out of five Broadening Top Patterns" (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high as quote; asserted)
  - pullback depth — **usually ≤ half, may reach two-thirds** — "it may not fail until it has regained two-thirds of the preceding decline, although it usually peters out around or even below the halfway mark." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - E&M concede the 3-peak rule is too strict — value: null — "The insistence that the third Top ... completes the Reversal Pattern may be regarded, in the light of experience, as setting too strict a limitation because Broadening Formations do, on occasion, go on to make a fourth and higher Top." — confidence(high)
  - **Right-Angled Broadening**: one boundary horizontal; still bearish — value: null — "Generally speaking, Right-Angled Broadening Formations carry Bearish implications, regardless of which side is horizontal, in nearly the same degree as the symmetrical manifestations." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - Right-Angled Broadening bullish exception — **roughly 3% close above the flat top on high volume** — "if prices finally burst up through that top line on high volume and close above it to a conclusive extent (roughly 3%), then it is safe to assume the preceding uptrend will be resumed and carried on for a worthwhile move. This does happen, although it is rare." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - the boundaries are loose — value: null — "these Broadening Patterns are characteristically loose and irregular, whereas Symmetrical Triangles are normally regular and compact. ... In a Broadening Formation, the rallies and declines usually do not all stop at clearly marked boundary lines." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high) — **this is the reason the pattern resists line-fitting.**
  - Murphy on tradability — value: null — "This is an unusually difficult pattern to trade and fortunately is relatively rare." (Fig 6.5 caption) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: **none measured; two asserted ratios ("9 times out of 10" bearish, "four out of five" pullback), both explicitly grounded in the authors' experience rather than a count, both without sample size, universe, period, or base rate.**
- **price_objective**: **Murphy gives none.** E&M give a comparative rather than a formula: "its measuring implications are probably no greater than those of a large, high-volume Head-and-Shoulders". So: `price_objective: null` with a comparative note only. **Do not encode a broadening-top target from these books.**
- **invalidation**: a fourth and higher top (E&M concede this happens); an upside break through a flat top by ≥3% on high volume for the right-angled form.
- **detection_notes**: Primitives: five ordered pivots H1 L1 H2 L2 H3 with `H1 < H2 < H3` and `L1 > L2` — **note this is the inverse ordering of the H&S and is trivially computable**; two diverging fitted lines (upper through the highs, lower through the lows) with a **positive divergence** test; a volume trend test with the sign *reversed* relative to every other consolidation pattern. E&M's warning that the swings "usually do not all stop at clearly marked boundary lines" means a strict line-fit residual test will reject genuine instances — the pivot-ordering test is the robust detector, the line fit is decoration. Computable from daily OHLCV throughout.
- **conflicts**: **This is the most flatly contradicted pattern in the file.**
  - **Edwards & Magee assert a 9-in-10 bearish bias.** Bulkowski (secondary, thepatternsite.com/bt.html) publishes **measured** statistics on broadening tops showing an upward-breakout population that is *larger and better-performing* than the downward one: **"18%/27%" break-even failure rate (up/down), "42%/13%" average rise/decline, "66%/42%" meeting the price target, "67%/67%" throwback/pullback**, from **1,215 up-breakout and 804 down-breakout "perfect" trades**. That is roughly 60% upward breakouts, with the up-breakout average move more than three times the down-breakout's. **E&M's "9 times out of 10 bearish" is not merely unmeasured; a later measured study points the other way.** Record both, do not merge, and do not build a bearish-only broadening screen citing "the classical canon" as if it were established.
  - Bulkowski also grades the pattern poorly overall ("the break even failure rate is above average and the average rise or decline is below average"), which agrees with **Murphy's** "unusually difficult pattern to trade" while disagreeing with **E&M's** confident bearish reading.
  - Note Bulkowski publishes no base rate either — no benchmark for what an arbitrary 42% rise rate would be over the same period — so his numbers are sampled but un-benchmarked.

## The Diamond

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 10 → *The Diamond*. **Murphy's *Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets* does not contain this pattern** — the only occurrence of "diamond" in the full text is a reference to the "Diamonds Trust" ETF. This is an outright coverage gap, not a disagreement.
- **definition**: "The Diamond Reversal Formation might be described either as a more or less Complex Head-and-Shoulders with a V-shaped neckline or as a Broadening Formation which, after two or three 'swings,' suddenly reverts into a regular Triangle that is nearly always of the Symmetrical form. So far as the accompanying volume pattern is concerned, the latter is ... possibly the better description; its name obviously derives from its pictorial resemblance to the conventional diamond shape."
- **criteria**:
  - two-phase structure — **broadening phase then symmetrical-triangle phase** — quoted above — confidence(high)
  - volume follows the *broadening-then-triangle* reading — value: null — "So far as the accompanying volume pattern is concerned, the latter is possibly the better description" (i.e. volume expands, then contracts) — confidence(high)
  - rarity and habitat — value: null — "the Diamond is not a common pattern. Since its development requires fairly active markets, it rarely occurs at Bottom Reversals. Its 'natural habitat' is Major Tops and the High-Volume Tops that precede extensive Intermediate Reactions." — confidence(high)
  - **E&M warn against over-detection** — value: null — "The reader is cautioned, however, against trying too hard to make Diamonds out of price patterns of the Head-and-Shoulders type. There is a temptation to do so because a V-shaped neckline may promise to give an earlier (and, hence, more profitable) breakout signal than the straight neckline of the Head-and-Shoulders. It is much safer to stick to the latter" — confidence(high)
  - the two conditions that permit the Diamond label — value: null — "unless the second half of the formation consists of a series of clean-cut, converging Minor Fluctuations, which plainly demands definition by converging boundary lines, and unless activity shows some tendency to diminish during this period" — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: **E&M do give one, and it is precise**: "Prices should move at least as far from the breakout point as the greatest width in points of the pattern from its Top (head) to Bottom (V in neckline). This, it must be emphasized, is a minimum rule and subject only to the usual qualification that a Reversal Formation must have something to reverse. Generally, the new trend carries prices eventually well beyond the minimum measurement."
- **invalidation**: failure of the second half to converge cleanly (in which case it is an H&S or a broadening top, not a diamond); the universal "something to reverse" prerequisite.
- **detection_notes**: **This is a two-phase shape and cannot be found by a single line-fit.** Primitives: split the candidate window at its widest point; run a **divergence** test on the left half and a **convergence** test on the right half, each with its own fitted upper/lower boundary; require the volume trend to be *up* then *down*. That is four fitted lines plus two volume-trend tests, with the split point as a free parameter — it is the most parameter-heavy detector in this file. E&M's own caution ("against trying too hard to make Diamonds") is a direct warning that the detector will over-fire; a screener should expect and cap the hit rate. Computable from daily OHLCV.
- **conflicts**: **Murphy omits the pattern entirely.** Against a third authority: Bulkowski (secondary, thepatternsite.com/diamondt.html) measures diamond tops at **"21%; 15%" break-even failure (up/down), "29%; 17%" average rise/decline, "65%; 63%" meeting the price target, "57%; 58%" throwback/pullback, on 733 "perfect" trades**, and states a measure rule that is **the same construction E&M give** (highest peak to lowest valley within the pattern, projected from the breakout) but **discounted by the historical hit rate** rather than taken at face value. E&M state the full height as a *minimum*; Bulkowski multiplies it by ~0.65. Record both — they are the same geometry with opposite calibration.

# PART C — CONTINUATION PATTERNS

## The Symmetrical Triangle (the Coil)

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 8 "Important Reversal Patterns: the Triangles" → *Symmetrical Triangles*, *Some cautions about Symmetrical Triangles*, *How prices break out of a Symmetrical Triangle*, *Reversal or Consolidation*, *Measuring implications of Triangles*. Murphy, Ch. 6 → *The Symmetrical Triangle* (p. 132).
- **definition**: E&M: "a sideways price area or trading range whose Top can be more or less accurately defined by a down-slanting boundary line and whose Bottom can be similarly bounded by an up-slanting line." Murphy: "shows two converging trendlines, the upper line descending and the lower line ascending. The vertical line at the left, measuring the height of the pattern, is called the base. The point of inter¬ section at the right, where the two lines meet, is called the apex."
- **criteria**:
  - **minimum reversal points — 4** — "The minimum requirement for a triangle is four reversal points. Remember that it always takes two points to draw a trendline. Therefore, in order to draw two converging trendlines, each line must be touched at least twice." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - E&M's identical requirement, spelled out as an ordering — **4, in strict order** — "before you can conclude that a Symmetrical Triangle is building, you must be able to see four Reversals of Minor Trend. If it comes after an advance in prices, you must first have a Top, next a Bottom, then a second Top lower than the first, and finally a second Bottom higher than the first Bottom (and prices must move up away from the second Bottom before you can be sure it is a Bottom)." (Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - typical reversal points — **6** — "While the minimum requirement is four reversal points, many triangles have six reversal points" (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - **breakout timing window — Murphy: two-thirds to three-quarters** — "As a general rule, prices should break out in the direction of the prior trend some¬ where between two-thirds to three-quarters of the horizontal width of the triangle. That is, the distance from the vertical base on the left of the pattern to the apex at the far right." — confidence(high)
  - Murphy's worked example — **20-week triangle ⇒ break in week 13–15** — "If the width, for example, were 20 weeks long, then the break¬ out should take place sometime between the 13th and the 15th week." — confidence(high)
  - Murphy's real example — **18-week triangle, broke week 13** — "Measured from left to right, the triangle width is 18 weeks. Prices broke out on the 13th week (see circle), just beyond the two-thirds point." (Fig 6.1d caption) — confidence(high)
  - **breakout timing window — Edwards & Magee: half to three-quarters** — "The best moves (up or down) seem to ensue when prices break out decisively at a point somewhere between half and three-quarters of the horizontal distance from the base (left-hand end) to the apex." — confidence(high) — **see conflicts.**
  - what happens past three-quarters — value: null — "If prices continue to move 'sideways' in narrower and narrower fluctuations from day to day after the three-quarter mark is passed, they are quite apt to keep right on to the apex and beyond in a dull drift" (E&M Ch.8); Murphy: "If prices remain within the triangle beyond the three-quarters point, the triangle begins to lose its potency" — confidence(high) — **the two books agree exactly on the upper bound and disagree on the lower.**
  - breakout margin — **roughly 3% close beyond the boundary (E&M)** — "the margin by which prices should close beyond the pattern lines is the same, roughly 3%." — confidence(high)
  - breakout margin — **a close, minimum (Murphy)** — "A minimum penetration criterion would be a closing price outside the trend- line and not just an intraday penetration." — confidence(high)
  - upside break needs volume, downside does not — value: null — "It is equally essential that an upside break in prices be confirmed by a marked increase in trading volume; lacking volume, do not trust the price achievement. But a downside breakout ... does not require confirmation by a pickup in activity." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - **heavy volume on a DOWN break is a red flag** — value: null — "The curious fact is a downside breakout from a Symmetrical Triangle attended to right from the start by conspicuously heavy volume is much more apt to be a false signal rather than the start of a genuine downtrend" (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - and it typically resolves as a 2–3 day shakeout — **two or three days** — "a high volume crack then frequently—we might even say usually—develops into a two- or three-day 'shakeout,' which quickly reverses itself and is followed by a genuine move in the up direction." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - continuation vs reversal split — **three out of four are continuations** — "Well, three out of four of these formations will turn out to be just that [a consolidation]; the fourth is the dangerous one (if you own the stock)." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high as quote; **asserted, no sample**)
  - reliability — **"more than two-thirds", explicitly uncounted** — "But most Symmetrical Triangles—**lacking an actual statistical count**, our experience would suggest more than two-thirds of them—behave themselves properly, produce no false signals that cannot be spotted before any damage is done." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high as quote; **the authors themselves flag it as not a measurement**)
  - **it is the worst offender for false moves** — value: null — "Symmetrical Triangles are subject to false moves to a far greater extent than the Head-and-Shoulders Formation or any of the other formations we have discussed or will discuss later. ... No technical chart formation is 100% reliable and, of all our present subject, is the worst offender." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - duration — **more than a month, generally less than three months** — "The triangle is considered an intermediate pattern, meaning that it usually takes longer than a month to form, but generally less than three months. A triangle that lasts less than a month is probably a different pattern, such as a pen¬ nant" (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - the apex becomes support/resistance — value: null — "The apex also acts as an important support or resistance level after the breakout occurs." (Murphy Ch.6); E&M: "The apex itself is a strong Support" — confidence(high)
  - direction is unknowable in advance — value: null — "There is seldom any clue given on the one chart containing the Triangle to tell in which direction prices are going to break out of the pattern until that action finally occurs." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: **none measured.** E&M's "more than two-thirds" carries the authors' own explicit disclaimer that no count was taken. The "three out of four are continuations" figure has no sample, universe, period, or base rate — and note that in a trending market the base rate for "the trend continues" is already high, so a 75% continuation rate is uninterpretable without knowing the unconditional continuation rate over the same universe. Neither book supplies it.
- **price_objective**: **Edwards & Magee (Ch.8) — a PARALLEL-LINE rule, not a base-height rule:** "Assuming we are dealing with an up-movement (upside breakout), draw from the Top of the first rally that initiated the pattern (in other words, from its upper left-hand corner) a line parallel to the Bottom boundary. This line will slope up away from the pattern to the right. Prices may be expected to climb until they reach this line. Also, as a rule, they will climb, following their breakout from the pattern, at about the same angle or rate as characterized their trend before entering the pattern. This principle permits us to arrive at an approximate time and level for them to attain the measuring line." E&M then downgrade it: "it is neither as definite nor as reliable as the Head-and-Shoulders formula."
  **Edwards & Magee (Ch.32) — a DIFFERENT, base-height rule:** "a decisive breakout from a Symmetrical Triangle is likely to carry at least as far as the height of the Triangle measured along its first reaction. This is a conservative measurement."
  **Murphy (Ch.6) — base-height preferred, parallel line as the alternative:** "The simplest technique is to measure the height of the ver¬ tical line at the widest part of the triangle (the base) and mea¬ sure that distance from the breakout point. Figure 6.2 shows the distance projected from the breakout point, which is the tech¬ nique I prefer. The second method is to draw a trendline from the top of the base (at point A) parallel to the lower trendline. This upper channel line then becomes the upside target in an uptrend."
- **invalidation**: prices drifting to and beyond the apex without a decisive break ("the triangle begins to lose its potency"); a return inside the pattern after a break (false move or premature breakout — indistinguishable at the time, per E&M).
- **detection_notes**: Computable. Primitives: ≥4 alternating pivots with `H2 < H1` and `L2 > L1`; two fitted lines with **negative** convergence (apex to the right); apex bar computed by line intersection; **`(breakout_bar - base_bar) / (apex_bar - base_bar)` as the timing ratio** — this is a genuinely computable and genuinely discriminating feature and both books hang a rule on it; close-vs-boundary penetration test; volume trend over the pattern (window unpublished). **The E&M parallel-line objective needs the line's slope, so the target is time-dependent — a screener that reports a single scalar target has silently chosen Murphy's rule, not E&M's Ch.8 rule.** The apex-as-support test after breakout is an extra primitive worth having.
- **conflicts**: **Three conflicts, one of them internal.**
  1. **Timing window: Murphy says two-thirds to three-quarters; Edwards & Magee say half to three-quarters.** Same upper bound, different lower bound. A screener filtering on `ratio >= 0.667` implements Murphy and discards E&M's best-performing 0.50–0.667 band.
  2. **Measured move: Edwards & Magee's Ch.8 rule is a sloping parallel line (a time-varying target); Murphy's preferred rule is a static base-height projection from the breakout point.** These are different objects, not restatements.
  3. **Internal to Edwards & Magee: Ch.8 gives the parallel-line rule, Ch.32 gives "the height of the Triangle measured along its first reaction."** The book contains two triangle measuring rules in two chapters and does not reconcile them. Record both.
  4. Against a third authority: Bulkowski (secondary, thepatternsite.com/st.html) measures **"25%/37%" break-even failure (up/down), "34%/12%" average rise/decline, "58%/36%" meeting the price target, "62%/65%" throwback/pullback, on "over 3,000 perfect trades"** — a measured 58%/36% target-attainment rate against E&M's asserted "more than two-thirds behave themselves". Not the same metric, but the direction is: the asserted figure is optimistic relative to the sampled one.

## The Ascending Triangle (Right-Angle Triangle, ascending form)

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 8 → *The Right-Angle Triangles*, *Volume characteristics same as the Symmetrical type*. Murphy, Ch. 6 → *The Ascending Triangle* (p. 136) and *The Ascending Triangle as a Bottom*.
- **definition**: Murphy: "the upper trendline is flat, while the lower line is rising. This pattern indicates that buyers are more aggressive than sellers." E&M: "If the top line is horizontal and the bottom line slopes up to meet it somewhere out to the right of the chart (at the apex), the Triangle is of the Ascending persuasion." E&M's supply-demand story: "pictures in the simplest and most normal form what happens when a growing demand for a certain stock meets a large block of shares for sale at a fixed price."
- **criteria**:
  - flat upper boundary + rising lower boundary — value: null (geometric) — quoted above — confidence(high)
  - **direction is known in advance (unlike the symmetrical)** — value: null — "No matter where in the trend structure the ascending or descending triangles appear, they have very definite forecasting implications. The ascending triangle is bullish and the descending triangle is bearish. The sym¬ metrical triangle, by contrast, is inherently a neutral pattern." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - completion — value: null — "The bullish breakout is signaled by a decisive closing above the flat upper trendline." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high); E&M apply the same ~3% margin as the symmetrical: "practically everything discussed about the Symmetrical Triangle will apply as well to the Right-Angle type."
  - **upside break MUST have volume** — value: null — "Upside breakouts (from an Ascending Pattern, of course) are attended by a conspicuous increase in trading volume; if not, they should be treated as suspect." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - volume within the pattern tilts to rallies — value: null — "In the Ascending Formation, there will usually be a pickup on each rally and an ebb in turnover on each decline within the pattern" (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - **false moves are rarer than in symmetricals** — value: null — "false moves from Right-Angle Formations are considerably rarer, it should be noted, than from Symmetrical" (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high) — **asserted, no count**
  - breakout timing — same window as symmetrical — value: null — "Good, reliable breakouts from Right-Angle Triangles usually occur at about the same stage of pattern completion as they do in Symmetrical Triangles. The earlier the breakout, the less apt it is to be a false move." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - it can be a bottom, and is still bullish — value: null — "It is not unusual toward the end of a down¬ trend to see an ascending triangle develop. However, even in this situation, the interpretation of the pattern is bullish." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - the flat line becomes support after the break — value: null — "That upper resistance line should act as support on subsequent dips after the breakout." (Murphy Ch.6, Fig 6.3a caption) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: **Murphy (Ch.6):** "Simply measure the height of the pattern at its widest point and project that vertical distance from the breakout point." Fig 6.3a caption: "The minimum price objective is obtained by measuring the height of the triangle (AB) and projecting that distance upward from the breakout point at C."
  **Edwards & Magee (Ch.8):** the parallel-line rule applies to right-angle triangles too — "one that applies to both the Symmetrical and the Right-Angle species". **Edwards & Magee (Ch.32):** "With Right-Angle Triangles, we also can take the long side (formed by the first reaction) as a rough measure of the minimum expected move."
- **invalidation**: a close below the rising lower boundary; drifting into the apex without a break.
- **detection_notes**: Computable and among the cleanest to implement. Primitives: ≥2 pivot highs within a tight horizontal band (**the "flat" tolerance is unpublished — E&M's 3% is stated for breakouts and for peak-equality in double/triple tops, not for the flat line here; borrowing it is your inference**); ≥2 pivot lows forming a positive-slope fit; convergence test. The volume tilt rule ("heavier on bounces, lighter on dips") is a genuinely computable and rarely-implemented feature: segment the pattern into up-legs and down-legs by pivot, and compare mean volume across the two sets. That is a real discriminator and needs no external window.
- **conflicts**: **On the measured move, Edwards & Magee themselves give two rules again** (Ch.8 parallel line; Ch.32 "the long side formed by the first reaction"), while Murphy gives the widest-height projection. The Ch.32 "long side / first reaction" and Murphy's "height at its widest point" coincide only when the first reaction *is* the widest point, which is typical but not guaranteed. Record both.

## The Descending Triangle (Right-Angle Triangle, descending form)

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 8 → *Descending Triangles*. Murphy, Ch. 6 → *The Descending Triangle* (p. 138) and *The Descending Triangle as a Top*.
- **definition**: E&M: "Descending Triangles have a horizontal lower boundary or Demand Line and a down-sloping upper boundary or Supply Line." Murphy: "the descending upper line and the flat lower line. This pat¬ tern indicates that sellers are more aggressive than buyers, and is usually resolved on the downside."
- **criteria**:
  - flat lower boundary + falling upper boundary — value: null (geometric) — confidence(high)
  - completion — value: null — "The downside signal is regis¬ tered by a decisive close under the lower trendline, usually on increased volume." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - **downside break does NOT require volume, but usually gets it by day 2–3** — **second or third day** — "Downside breakouts (from Descending Patterns) may not evince much of a pickup in activity, but turnover usually speeds up the second or third day out of pattern." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high)
  - volume within the pattern tilts to declines — value: null — "In the descending formation, volume should be heavier on the downside and lighter during the bounces." (Murphy Ch.6); E&M: "in the Descending Formation, the opposite is true, but sometimes it is not quite so evident." — confidence(high)
  - throwback likelihood depends on the general market — value: null — "if prices break down out of a Descending Triangle in an individual stock at a time when the rest of the market is firm, a Pullback Rally is fairly certain to intervene before any extensive further decline takes place." (E&M Ch.8) — confidence(high) — **this criterion is NOT computable from the single stock's OHLCV; it requires an index series.**
  - it can be a top — value: null — "it is not unusual on occasion for the descending triangle to be found at market tops. ... In that case, a close below the flat lower line would sig¬ nal a major trend reversal to the downside." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published. E&M assert: "their implications are equally strong and their failures equally rare" (relative to the ascending form) — no count.
- **price_objective**: Murphy: "the analyst must measure the height of the pattern at the base to the left and then project that distance down from the breakdown point." Fig 6.4a caption: "The measuring tech¬ nique is the height of the triangle (AB) projected down from the breakout at point C." E&M: the parallel-line rule, measured "down, of course, from the lower left corner".
- **invalidation**: a close above the falling upper boundary; apex drift.
- **detection_notes**: Mirror of the ascending case. **The one criterion here that is explicitly NOT computable from the stock's own daily OHLCV is E&M's market-context throwback rule** — it requires a concurrent index/breadth series, which is a cross-series join, not a per-symbol computation. Flag it as such; it is the only place in either book where a pattern rule depends on another instrument.
- **conflicts**: none between the two books beyond the shared triangle measuring-rule divergence noted above.

## The Rectangle Formation (trading range, congestion area, Dow "line")

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 9 → *The Rectangles, Double and Triple Tops* (and the comparison bullet list). Murphy, Ch. 6 → *The Rectangle Formation* (p. 147).
- **definition**: E&M: "A Rectangle consists of a series of sideways price fluctuations, a 'trading area,' as it is sometimes called, which can be bounded both top and bottom by horizontal lines." Murphy: "It represents a pause in the trend dur¬ ing which prices move sideways between two parallel horizontal lines. ... In Dow Theory parlance, it is referred to as a line."
- **criteria**:
  - both boundaries horizontal — value: null — confidence(high); E&M allow slight tilt: "On rare occasions, you may discover a chart pattern whose upper and lower boundary lines are parallel but either slightly down-sloping or up-sloping. So long as their departure from the horizontal is trivial, they may be treated as Rectangles." — **missing**: "trivial" is unquantified.
  - E&M allow slight convergence too — value: null — "patterns whose boundaries, while nearly horizontal, tend somewhat to converge. These may be considered Rectangles or Symmetrical Triangles; it does not matter which because the 'prognosis' will be the same in either case." — confidence(high)
  - completion — value: null — "A decisive close outside either the upper or lower bound¬ ary signals completion of the rectangle" (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high); E&M: "Breakouts—Here also the same rules apply as with Triangles. Review volume requirements, margin of penetration, and so on thereunder." (i.e. the ~3% margin)
  - **volume behaves differently from other consolidations** — value: null — "The vol¬ ume pattern differs from other continuation patterns in the sense that the broad price swings prevent the usual dropoff in activity seen in other such patterns." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high) — but note **E&M say the opposite**, see conflicts.
  - the volume tilt discriminates continuation from reversal — value: null — "If the rallies are on heavier and the setbacks on lighter volume, then the formation is probably a continuation in the uptrend. If the heavier volume is on the downside, then it can be considered a warning of a possible trend reversal in the works." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - duration — **one to three months** — "In terms of duration, the rectangle usually falls into the one to three month category, similar to triangles and wedges." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - **pullback/throwback frequency — about 40%, within three days to three weeks** — "Our estimate would be that a Pullback or Throwback ... occurs within three days to three weeks in about 40% of all cases." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high as quote; **labelled an estimate**)
  - **false moves are rarer than in symmetrical triangles** — value: null — "False moves—Much less frequent from Rectangles than from Symmetrical Triangles. A clearly defined Rectangle is, in fact, almost as reliable as a Head-and-Shoulders, although not as powerful in its implications." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high) — **asserted, no count**
  - premature breakouts are slightly more frequent — value: null — "Premature breakouts—Slightly more frequent, perhaps, from Rectangles than from Triangles." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high)
  - continuation vs reversal ratio — value: null — "The Rectangle is more often a Consolidation Formation than a Reversal Formation, the ratio being about the same as with Symmetrical Triangles." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high) — **cross-reference: that ratio is E&M's asserted "three out of four".**
  - as a reversal it favours bottoms — value: null — "As Reversal Patterns, Rectangles appear more frequently at Bottoms (either Major or Intermediate) than at Tops. Long, thin, dull Rectangles are not uncommon at Primary Bottoms" (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high)
  - **squat rectangles outperform long thin ones** — value: null — "the brief, wide-swinging forms, which appear nearly square in shape on the chart and in which turnover is active, are more dynamic than the longer and narrower manifestations. Moves out of the latter almost always hesitate or react at the 'minimum' point before carrying on." (E&M Ch.9) — confidence(high) — **this is an aspect-ratio rule and it is scale-dependent, i.e. "nearly square on the chart" is not computable without fixing the chart scaling.**
- **measured_performance**: none measured. The 40% throwback figure is explicitly "our estimate". "Almost as reliable as a Head-and-Shoulders" is a comparison between two unmeasured assertions.
- **price_objective**: **Edwards & Magee (Ch.9):** "A safe minimum measuring formula for the Rectangle is given by its width. Prices should go at least as far in points beyond the pattern as the difference in points between the top and bottom lines of the pattern itself, though they may go much farther." (**Note the OCR/terminology trap: E&M say "width" but define it as the top-to-bottom difference, i.e. what everyone else calls the height.**)
  **Edwards & Magee (Ch.32):** "with Rectangles the minimum we may reasonably expect after a breakout is a distance equal to the height of the Rectangle."
  **Murphy (Ch.6):** "The most common measuring technique applied to the rectangle is based on the height of the price range. Measure the height of the trading range, from top to bottom, and then project that vertical distance from the breakout point."
  **All three statements are the same rule.** This is the one measured-move formula on which E&M (both chapters) and Murphy fully agree.
- **invalidation**: a close back inside after a break; the pattern turning into a triple top (Murphy warns explicitly: "the trader must always be alert for signs that it may turn into a reversal pattern, such as a triple bottom").
- **detection_notes**: Computable and simple. Primitives: two horizontal levels fitted to pivot highs and pivot lows with a band tolerance (**tolerance unpublished — "trivial departure from the horizontal" has no number**); ≥2 touches per boundary (implied by the trendline rule); a close-beyond test with the penetration filter; duration bounds 1–3 months; a per-leg volume comparison (up-legs vs down-legs) for the continuation/reversal discriminator. **The aspect-ratio rule ("nearly square on the chart") is NOT computable from OHLCV** — squareness is a rendering property. If you want it, you must define your own price-per-bar normalisation and label it your own.
- **conflicts**: **On volume during formation, Murphy and Edwards & Magee flatly contradict each other.** Murphy: "The vol¬ ume pattern differs from other continuation patterns in the sense that the broad price swings prevent the usual dropoff in activity seen in other such patterns." Edwards & Magee: "Volume—Follows the same rules as in the Triangles, **gradually diminishing** as the Rectangle lengthens. Any contrary development, unless it be a momentary news flurry, is suspect." **One book says volume should NOT dry up in a rectangle; the other says it should, and that failure to dry up is suspect.** A volume-contraction gate on rectangles implements E&M and contradicts Murphy. Record both; do not merge.

## Flags

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 11 "Consolidation Formations" → *Flags and Pennants*, *The measuring formula*, *Reliability of Flags and Pennants*. Murphy, Ch. 6 → *Flags and Pennants* (p. 141).
- **definition**: E&M: "a small, compact parallelogram of price fluctuations, or a tilted Rectangle that slopes back moderately against the prevailing trend." Murphy: "The flag resembles a parallelogram or rectangle marked by two parallel trendlines that tend to slope against the prevailing trend. In a downtrend, the flag would have a slight upward slope."
- **criteria**:
  - **prerequisite: a near-vertical prior move (the flagpole)** — value: null — "one of the requirements for both the flag and the pennant is that they be preceded by a sharp and almost straight line move." (Murphy Ch.6); E&M test 1: "The Consolidation (Flag or Pennant) should occur after a 'straight-line' move." — confidence(high) — **missing**: no steepness metric, no minimum percentage move, no maximum bar count for the pole.
  - the flag slopes AGAINST the trend — value: null — "The flag should slope against the trend." (Murphy Ch.6, Fig 6.6a caption); E&M allow exceptions: "the very short and 'solid' ones will frequently develop horizontally and look like small squares. (On rare occasions, a pattern of the Flag type in an uptrend will even slope up a trifle.)" — confidence(high)
  - **volume must dry up and keep drying up** — value: null — E&M test 2: "Activity should diminish appreciably and constantly during the pattern's construction and continue to decline until prices break away from it." — confidence(high) — **missing**: no ratio.
  - **duration — Murphy: one to three weeks** — "Both patterns are relatively short term and should be com¬ pleted within one to three weeks. Pennants and flags in down¬ trends tend to take even less time to develop, and often last no longer than one or two weeks." — confidence(high)
  - **duration — Edwards & Magee: five days to three weeks typical, hard limit four weeks, suspicion past three** — "This process of Minor Fluctuations may continue for only five days to a week if the Flag is narrow, or it can go on for as long as three weeks." and test 3: "Prices should break away (in the expected direction) in not more than four weeks. A pattern of this type that extends beyond three weeks should be watched with suspicion." — confidence(high)
  - E&M's Ch.32 restatement of the time limit as a **sell** rule — **about three weeks** — "If the congestion continues or sags off for more than about three weeks, sell the stock; it is probably not the real thing." — confidence(high)
  - internal swing rhythm — **three or four days per leg, rarely more** — "Sometimes each rally and setback within the Flag takes three or four days, rarely more." (E&M Ch.11) — confidence(high)
  - completion — value: null — "Both patterns are completed on the penetration of the upper trendline in an uptrend. The breaking of those trendlines should take place on heavier volume." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - **it flies at half-mast** — value: null — "Flags and pennants are said to 'fly at half-mast' from a flagpole. ... The term 'half-mast' suggests that these minor continuation patterns tend to appear at about the halfway point of the move." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published. Both books assert unusually high reliability without a count. Murphy: "Flags and pennants are among the most reliable of contin¬ uation patterns and only rarely produce a trend reversal." E&M: "These pretty little patterns of Consolidation are justly regarded as among the most dependable of chart formations, both as to directional and measuring indications. They do fail occasionally, but almost never without giving warning before the pattern itself is completed." **No sample, no rate, no base rate.**
- **price_objective**: **Edwards & Magee (Ch.11), the more precise of the two:** "go back to the beginning of that immediately preceding move, to the point at which it broke away from a previous Consolidation or Reversal Formation (or through a significant trendline or Resistance Level ...), a point recognizable as a rule by a quick spurt in activity, and measure from there to the Minor Reversal level at which the Flag or Pennant started to form. Then measure the same distance from the point at which prices break out of the Flag or Pennant, and in the same direction. The level thus arrived at is the minimum expectation of this type of Consolidation Pattern."
  **E&M add a scaling caveat that matters for implementation:** "advances from Flags or Pennants in an uptrend generally go farther (in terms of points or dollars) than the preceding move, whereas declines may not carry quite so far. Hence, the formula is best applied on a semilogarithmic chart by measuring actual chart distance rather than by counting points."
  **Murphy (Ch.6):** "measure the distance of the preceding move from the original breakout point. That is to say, the point at which the original trend signal was given, either by the penetra- lon of a support or resistance level or an important trendline. That vertical distance of the preceding move is then measured from the breakout point of the flag or pennant"
  **These two are the same rule.** Both anchor the pole at the *prior breakout*, not at a swing low — a distinction most modern implementations get wrong.
- **invalidation**: exceeding the duration limit (E&M: sell it); volume failing to contract; a break in the wrong direction. E&M: "They do fail occasionally, but almost never without giving warning before the pattern itself is completed."
- **detection_notes**: Computable, and the pole anchor is the crux. Primitives: (1) **a prior breakout event** — the pole must be measured from "the point at which the original trend signal was given", which means the flag detector **depends on a prior pattern/level-break detector**, not merely on a swing low. This is the only measured-move rule in the canon with a *cross-pattern dependency*, and getting it wrong systematically shortens every target. (2) A steepness test on the pole (threshold yours, not the books'). (3) Two parallel fitted lines over ≤15–20 bars with slope opposing the pole. (4) A strictly-decreasing volume test over the flag ("appreciably and constantly"). (5) A duration cap (3 weeks warn, 4 weeks reject). (6) **E&M's semilog caveat means the target should be computed as a log-price distance, not a point distance** — implementing it in points will systematically undershoot upside targets and overshoot downside ones, which is exactly the bias E&M predict.
- **conflicts**:
  - **Duration: Murphy caps at three weeks; Edwards & Magee cap at four with suspicion past three.** A `<= 15 bars` filter implements Murphy; `<= 20 bars` implements E&M.
  - **Point vs log measurement: E&M explicitly prefer semilog; Murphy states the rule in points with no caveat.** These give different targets on any move of size.
  - Against a third authority: Bulkowski (secondary, thepatternsite.com/flags.html) measures flags at **"44%/45%" break-even failure rate (up/down), "9%/8%" average rise/decline, "46%/46%" meeting the price target** — with the caveat that "Performance results for flags are based on the short-term price swing, not the change from the breakout to the ultimate high or low as in most other chart patterns." **A ~45% break-even failure rate is a direct measured contradiction of "among the most dependable of chart formations" and "only rarely produce a trend reversal."** The metrics are not identical (Bulkowski measures the short-term swing), so this is a partial rather than a clean refutation — but the gap between "most dependable" and a 45% failure rate is far too large to be explained by definition drift alone. Record both.

## Pennants

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 11 → *The Pennant: a pointed Flag*, *The measuring formula*. Murphy, Ch. 6 → *Flags and Pennants* (p. 141).
- **definition**: E&M: "The only important difference between a Pennant and a Flag is the former is bounded by converging boundary lines rather than parallel lines. The normal Pennant, in other words, is a small, compact, sloping Triangle. It slants down when it appears in an uptrend". Murphy: "The pennant is identified by two converging trendlines and is more horizontal. It very closely resembles a small symmet¬ rical triangle."
- **criteria**:
  - converging (not parallel) boundaries — value: null — confidence(high)
  - **the discriminator against a symmetrical triangle is DURATION** — value: null — "A triangle that lasts less than a month is probably a different pattern, such as a pen¬ nant" (Murphy Ch.6); Fig 6.6b caption: "Resembles a small symmetrical trian¬ gle, but usually lasts no longer than three weeks." — confidence(high)
  - the same duration limits as flags — **one to three weeks (Murphy) / four-week hard cap (E&M)** — confidence(high)
  - volume must dry up — value: null — "An important requirement is that volume should dry up noticeably while each of the patterns is forming." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - orientation — **Murphy: "more horizontal"; E&M: "slants down ... in an uptrend"** — value: null — confidence(high) — **see conflicts.**
  - E&M's discriminator against a Wedge — **three weeks** — "It (the Wedge) normally takes more than three weeks to complete; a shorter pattern of this shape is nearly always better classified as a Pennant" (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published (shares the flag's asserted reliability).
- **price_objective**: E&M: "The same approximate measuring formula applies to the Pennant as to the Flag. They are both 'Half-Mast' Patterns". Murphy: "The measuring implications are similar for both patterns." Fig 6.6b caption: "The move after the pennant is completed should dupli¬ cate the size of the move preceding it."
- **invalidation**: as for flags; plus reclassification — a converging pattern lasting >3 weeks is a wedge (E&M) or a triangle (Murphy).
- **detection_notes**: Computable. Same as the flag but with a convergence test instead of a parallelism test. **The critical engineering point is that pennant / symmetrical triangle / wedge are distinguished ONLY by duration and slope, not by shape** — a converging two-line pattern is a pennant if short, a symmetrical triangle if long and unsloped, and a wedge if long and sloped. A pattern library needs an explicit precedence chain (duration → slope) or it will emit all three labels on the same bars. Neither book states that chain as a rule; it has to be assembled from three separate remarks in two different chapters.
- **conflicts**: **On slope, Murphy and Edwards & Magee disagree.** Murphy: the pennant "is more horizontal". E&M: "It slants down when it appears in an uptrend" — i.e. sloping against the trend, like a flag. A screener requiring a horizontal pennant implements Murphy; one requiring a counter-trend slope implements E&M.

## The Wedge Formation (Rising Wedge and Falling Wedge)

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 10 → *Wedge Formations*, *The Falling Wedge*, *Wedges on weekly and monthly charts*, *Rising Wedges common in Bear Market Rallies*. Murphy, Ch. 6 → *The Wedge Formation* (p. 146).
- **definition**: E&M: "The Wedge is a chart formation in which the price fluctuations are confined within converging straight (or practically straight) lines but differs from a Triangle in that **both boundary lines either slope up or slope down**. ... In a Rising Wedge, both boundary lines slant up from left to right, but because the two lines converge, the lower must project at a steeper angle than the upper. In a Falling Wedge, the opposite is true."
- **criteria**:
  - **both boundaries slope the same way, and converge** — value: null (geometric, fully computable) — quoted above — confidence(high)
  - **the maxim** — value: null — "a rising wedge is bearish and a falling wedge is bullish." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high); E&M concur: "the market analyst should always be guided by the general maxim that a rising wedge is bearish and a falling wedge is bullish."
  - it slopes against the prevailing trend when acting as a continuation — value: null — "As a rule, like the flag pattern, the wedge slants against the prevailing trend." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high)
  - **duration — Murphy: more than one month, not more than three** — "the wedge usually lasts more than one month but not more than three months, putting it into the inter¬ mediate category." — confidence(high)
  - **duration — Edwards & Magee: more than three weeks, seldom longer than three months** — "It (the Wedge) normally takes more than three weeks to complete; a shorter pattern of this shape is nearly always better classified as a Pennant" and "Most true Wedges are too short-lived (seldom longer than three months) to take on a recognizable definition on a monthly chart" — confidence(high) — **see conflicts on the lower bound.**
  - **prices stay inside for at least two-thirds of the base-to-apex distance** — **two-thirds** — "Prices almost always fluctuate within the Wedge's confines for at least two-thirds of the distance from the base (beginning of convergence) to the apex; in many cases, they rise clear to the apex, and in some, they actually go a short distance beyond, pushing on out at the Top in a last-gasp rally before collapsing." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high) — **note this is the OPPOSITE prescription to the triangle, where a break past three-quarters is a warning sign.**
  - volume contracts toward the apex — value: null — "Trading volume in a Wedge tends to follow the regular Triangle Pattern, diminishing gradually as prices move up toward the apex of the Wedge." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - the aftermath differs by direction — value: null — "When prices break out of a Rising Wedge, they usually fall away rapidly, but when they move out of a Falling Wedge, they are more apt to drift sideways or in a dull 'Saucering-around' movement before they begin to rise. The Rising Wedge may, therefore, call for quick action to secure profits, whereas with a Falling Wedge, the trader ordinarily can take his time" (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - **a nearly-horizontal boundary reclassifies it as a triangle** — value: null — "If one boundary line is nearly horizontal, or if the daily closing prices tend to fall at about the same level, then the formation is more safely construed as a Triangle." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no angle threshold for "nearly horizontal".
  - **rising wedges are a bear-market rally signature** — value: null — "the Rising Wedge is a quite characteristic pattern for Bear Market Rallies. It is so typical, in fact, that frequent appearance of Wedges at a time when, after an extensive decline, there is some question as to whether a new Bull Trend is in the making may be taken as evidence that the Primary Trend is still down." and "A Rising Wedge on an arithmetically scaled weekly chart is almost invariably a Bear Market phenomenon" (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high)
  - **the arithmetic-scale artefact warning** — value: null — "an entire Major Bear Decline on any arithmetic monthly chart may appear like a giant Falling Wedge. This is due to the fact that the up and down fluctuations that compose the Major Swing, while maintaining about the same extent in percentage, tend to shorten in terms of points (dollars) as prices move from higher to lower levels. Such Major chart patterns are not the true Wedges we have discussed herein. When plotted on semilogarithmic scale, these same moves would normally show a Parallel or even slightly widening, instead of Converging, Channel." (E&M Ch.10) — confidence(high) — **this is the most important implementation warning in the entire canon; see detection_notes.**
- **measured_performance**: none published by either book.
- **price_objective**: **Neither book gives a wedge measuring formula.** Murphy gives none. E&M give only a retracement expectation, not a projection: "The ensuing drop ordinarily retraces all of the ground gained within the Wedge itself, and sometimes more." So: `price_objective: null`, with E&M's retracement-of-the-wedge as the closest available substitute — and it is a *retracement*, not a projection, i.e. the target is the wedge's own base, not base-height projected beyond the breakout.
- **invalidation**: reclassification (a horizontal boundary ⇒ triangle; duration <3 weeks ⇒ pennant); a break in the direction of the slope rather than against it.
- **detection_notes**: Computable — with one enormous caveat.
  Primitives: two fitted boundaries with the **same sign of slope** and convergence; a `(bars_elapsed / bars_to_apex) >= 0.667` dwell test; a volume-contraction test; a slope-vs-prior-trend sign test.
  **THE CAVEAT — and it invalidates naive implementations: Edwards & Magee state explicitly that falling wedges are MANUFACTURED BY ARITHMETIC PRICE SCALING.** Because a constant *percentage* swing shrinks in *points* as price falls, any extended decline plotted arithmetically converges by construction. **A detector that fits lines to raw prices will emit falling wedges on essentially every sustained downtrend, and they will be artefacts, not patterns.** The fix E&M name is to work in log space: fit the boundaries to `log(price)`, where a constant-percentage decline gives parallel or widening lines and only a genuine wedge converges. **This is the single highest-value, most-ignored implementation instruction in either book, and it applies to the falling wedge far more severely than to any other pattern.** It also means a wedge detector and a triangle detector must run on *different* transforms of the same series if both are to be faithful.
- **conflicts**:
  - **Duration lower bound: Murphy says more than one month; Edwards & Magee say more than three weeks.** The gap (3–4 weeks) is exactly where E&M would say "wedge" and Murphy would say "pennant".
  - **Neither book supplies a measured move**, which puts the wedge in the same category as the saucer and the V — a pattern with a direction but no published target. Any wedge target in a screener is imported from elsewhere.
  - E&M's arithmetic-scale warning is unique to them; **Murphy gives no scaling caveat at all**, so a reader following Murphy alone will build the artefact-generating detector.

## The Measured Move (the swing measurement)

- **origin / source_name**: Murphy, Ch. 6 → *The Measured Move* (p. 151). **Edwards & Magee do not treat this as a named pattern**, though the equivalent appears in their retracement discussion (Ch.3, one-third/two-thirds) and their Ch.32 summary.
- **definition**: Murphy: "The measured move, or the swing measurement as it is sometimes called, describes the phenomenon where a major market advance or decline is divided into two equal and parallel moves."
- **criteria**:
  - **the second leg duplicates the first in size AND slope** — value: null — "This theory holds that the second leg in the advance (CD) duplicates the size and slope of the first leg (AB)." (Murphy Fig 6.10a caption) — confidence(high)
  - **the corrective wave retraces a third to a half** — **1/3 to 1/2** — "The corrective wave (BC) often retraces a third to a half of AB before the uptrend is resumed." (Murphy Fig 6.10a caption); restated in the text: "a countertrend swing from point B to point C (which retraces a third to a half of wave AB)" — confidence(high)
  - the market must be orderly — value: null — "For this approach to work, the market moves should be fairly orderly and well defined." (Murphy Ch.6) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no orderliness metric.
  - Edwards & Magee's related (but different) retracement rule — **one-third to two-thirds, typically ~50%** — "Normally, they retrace from one-third to two-thirds of the gain (or loss ...) in prices registered in the preceding swing in the Primary Direction." and "Note, however, the one-third/two-thirds rule is not an unbreakable law; it is simply a statement of probabilities. Most Secondaries are confined within these limits; many of them stop very close to the halfway mark, retracing 50% of the preceding Primary" (E&M Ch.3) — confidence(high) — **see conflicts.**
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: Murphy Fig 6.10b caption, with a worked example: "A measured move takes the prior upleg (AB) and adds that value to the bottom of the correction at C. On this chart, the prior uptrend (AB) was 20 points. Adding that to the lowpoint at C (62) yielded a price target to 82 (D)." Text: "The height of wave (AB), therefore, is simply measured upward from the bottom of the correction at point C."
- **invalidation**: a correction deeper than the stated retracement band; a disorderly move.
- **detection_notes**: Computable from daily OHLCV. Primitives: three pivots A, B, C with `C` between `A` and `B` in price; a retracement ratio `(B-C)/(B-A)` tested against the band; the target `C + (B-A)`. **The "duplicates the size AND SLOPE" clause is the part usually dropped** — enforcing it means also testing `(bars C→D) ≈ (bars A→B)`, which turns a price target into a *price-and-time* target and materially reduces hits. Both are computable; the slope clause is sourced and should not be silently discarded.
- **conflicts**: **Murphy's measured-move retracement band is one-third to one-half; Edwards & Magee's secondary-reaction band is one-third to two-thirds.** These are not quite the same object (Murphy is describing a tradeable three-leg structure, E&M are describing secondary reactions in Dow terms), but a screener that filters retracements will pick one band and inherit that book's population. Record both. Note also **Murphy uses the 1/2-to-2/3 band elsewhere** for the H&S right shoulder ("often retrace one-half to two-thirds of the decline from points C to D"), so Murphy himself uses two different bands in two chapters.

# PART D — GAPS AS MULTI-BAR STRUCTURE

## Breakaway gaps

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 12 "Gaps" → *Breakaway gaps*. Murphy, Ch. 4 → *Price Gaps* → *The Breakaway Gap* (p. 94).
- **definition**: Murphy: "The breakaway gap usually occurs at the completion of an important price pattern, and usually signals the beginning of a significant market move." E&M: "it develops at the completion of the formation in the breakaway move."
- **criteria**:
  - location — **at the completion of a price pattern** — quoted above — confidence(high)
  - **E&M: almost every decisive break from a horizontal congestion gaps, but many are invisible on daily bars** — value: null — "gaps of this type actually occur on almost every decisive breakout from a Horizontal Congestion, although many of them do not show on the charts because they occur during a day and not between one day's close and the following day's opening." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **this is a direct statement that daily OHLCV under-detects breakaway gaps.**
  - volume — value: null — "Breakaway gaps usually occur on heavy volume." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high) — **missing**: no threshold.
  - fill behaviour — value: null — "More often than not, breakaway gaps are not filled. Prices may return to the upper end of the gap (in the case of a bullish breakout), and may even close a portion of the gap, but some portion of the gap is often left unfilled. As a rule, the heavier the volume after such a gap appears, the less likely it is to be filled." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high) — **"more often than not" is the only frequency given, and it has no sample.**
  - the gap becomes support — value: null — "Upside gaps usually act as sup¬ port areas on subsequent market corrections. It's important that prices not fall below gaps during an uptrend. In all cases a close below an upward gap is a sign of weakness." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
  - E&M's mechanism — value: null — the "vacuum" on the order book above a flat-topped pattern: "the next buyer of the stock finds none offered at 40.125 or 40.25 and he has to bid up a point or more to get his shares, thus creating a Breakaway Gap." (Ch.12) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: none. The breakaway gap carries no measuring implication in either book; E&M say explicitly that only the runaway gap does: "The only type of gap that carries substantial implications as to the extent of the move to follow is the Runaway or Continuation Gap." (Ch.32)
- **invalidation**: a close back below (above) the gap.
- **detection_notes**: Computable from daily OHLCV: `low[t] > high[t-1]` (up gap) or `high[t] < low[t-1]` (down gap), **conditioned on the gap bar being the breakout bar of a detected pattern** — the classification is entirely contextual, so a gap detector alone cannot label a gap breakaway. **E&M's warning that most such gaps are intraday and therefore invisible on daily bars means the daily-bar detector systematically under-counts this pattern and that is a known, sourced limitation, not a bug.** Intraday data would be required to match E&M's population.
- **conflicts**: none between the two books.

## Continuation / Runaway / Measuring gaps

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 12 → *Continuation or runaway gaps and the measuring rule*, *Two or more runaway gaps*. Murphy, Ch. 4 → *The Runaway or Measuring Gap*.
- **definition**: Murphy: "After the move has been under¬ way for awhile, somewhere around the middle of the move, prices will leap forward to form a second type of gap (or a series of gaps) called the runaway gap. This type of gap reveals a situation where the market is moving effortlessly on moderate volume." E&M: it occurs "at just about the halfway point between the breakout that inaugurated the move and the Reversal Day or Congestion Pattern that calls an end to it."
- **criteria**:
  - location — **approximately the halfway point of the move** — quoted above, both books — confidence(high)
  - **volume is MODERATE, not heavy** — value: null — "the market is moving effortlessly on moderate volume" (Murphy Ch.4); E&M: "when quotations are moving most rapidly and easily with relation to the volume of transactions" — confidence(high) — **this is the discriminator against the exhaustion gap and it is a price-move-per-unit-volume ratio, which IS computable.**
  - it is not associated with an area pattern — value: null — "The Runaway Gap, on the other hand, as well as the Exhaustion Gap ... is not associated with Area Patterns, but occurs in the course of rapid, straight-line advances or declines." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high)
  - it usually is not filled — value: null — "Runaway Gaps are usually not covered for a considerable length of time, as a rule, not until the market stages a swing of Major or full Intermediate proportions in the opposite direction." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high)
  - **the first gap in a fast move is presumed a runaway** — value: null — "you may ordinarily assume (unless the contrary appears from other and weightier indications) the first gap in a rapid advance or decline is a Continuation Gap, but each succeeding gap must be regarded with more and more suspicion, especially if it is wider than its predecessor." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **this is an explicitly ORDINAL rule and is fully computable.**
  - a close below it is bearish — value: null — "As in the case of the breakaway, a close below the runaway gap is a negative sign in an uptrend." (Murphy Ch.4) — confidence(high)
  - multiple runaway gaps complicate the midpoint — value: null — "No quick and easy rule can be laid down, but studious inspection of the chart, especially of the volume trend, will usually afford an answer." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **missing**: E&M explicitly decline to give a rule for locating the midpoint when there are two or three gaps.
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: **Edwards & Magee (Ch.12), the crisper statement:** "Its inference is that prices will go as much farther beyond the gap as they already have gone between the beginning of the move and the gap, as measured directly (and vertically) on the chart."
  **E&M's scaling caveat, again:** "Since there is a tendency for advances to run, in terms of points, beyond the price levels arithmetically implied by this rule, and for declines to be more strictly limited, the gap-measuring rule works out particularly well when applied directly on semilogarithmic scale charts. On arithmetic charts, look for a trifle more on the upside and a trifle less on the downside. (In any event, you will be wise to 'bank' on something short of the theoretical goal.)"
  **Murphy (Ch.4):** "By measur¬ ing the distance the trend has already traveled, from the original trend signal or breakout, an estimate of the probable extent of the remaining move can be determined by doubling the amount already achieved."
  **Same rule, both books. This is the one gap with a measured move, and it is the one place where E&M and Murphy fully agree on both the anchor and the arithmetic.**
- **invalidation**: a close back through the gap; reclassification as an exhaustion gap (see next).
- **detection_notes**: Computable. Primitives: gap detection; a "rapid straight-line move" context test (steepness — threshold yours); **the move's origin anchor is "the original trend signal or breakout"**, which — exactly as with the flag — means this detector depends on a prior breakout detector, not on a swing low; gap ordinality within the move (first / second / third); gap width relative to the prior gap; **a price-change-per-unit-volume ratio** to distinguish "moving effortlessly on moderate volume" from the exhaustion case. **E&M's semilog preference applies here too**: compute the target in log space or accept the upside undershoot they predict. E&M's refusal to give a multi-gap midpoint rule is a genuine `value: null` — do not invent one.
- **conflicts**: none on the formula. Only E&M supply the semilog caveat.

## Exhaustion gaps

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 12 → *Exhaustion gaps*. Murphy, Ch. 4 → *The Exhaustion Gap*.
- **definition**: Murphy: "The final type of gap appears near the end of a market move. ... Near the end of an uptrend, prices leap forward in a last gasp, so to speak. However, that upward leap quickly fades and prices turn lower within a couple of days or within a week." E&M: "In such moves, a wide gap may appear at the very end, that is, between the next to the last and the last day of the move."
- **criteria**:
  - **the day-after test — extraordinary volume with disproportionate price progress** — value: null — "If trading activity mounts to an extraordinary height during the session following the gap, and particularly if the previous trend in prices does not appear to be carried along at a pace commensurate with that day's activity, the gap is probably of the Exhaustion class." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **this is a price-per-volume ratio test and is computable; "extraordinary" has no threshold.**
  - **the confirming test — a reversal day closing back near the gap edge** — value: null — "This interpretation is reinforced, in fact, made a virtual certainty, if the day after, the gap develops into a Reversal Day ... with the closing price registered back near the edge of the gap." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high)
  - **the fill timing — most often within two to five days** — **2–5 days** — "But Exhaustion Gaps are quickly closed, most often within two to five days, a fact that furnishes a final clue to distinguish Exhaustion from Continuation" (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **this is one of the very few hard numeric windows in the canon and it is fully computable.**
  - Murphy's version of the same window — **a couple of days or within a week** — "that upward leap quickly fades and prices turn lower within a couple of days or within a week. When prices close under that last gap, it is usually a dead giveaway that the exhaus¬ tion gap has made its appearance." — confidence(high)
  - **it is seldom the first gap** — value: null — "An Exhaustion Gap is seldom the first gap in a runaway move; it is usually preceded by at least one Continuation Gap." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high)
  - width is relative and undefined — value: null — "Width is, of necessity, relative in this study; it is impossible to lay down any exact rules to define wide or narrow." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **the authors explicitly refuse to publish a threshold; do not invent one and attribute it to them.**
  - the prior pattern's target tells you which it is — value: null — "If the trend has already carried out the full implications of the price formation or Congestion Area from which it emerged, Exhaustion is more likely than Continuation. By the same token, if the reasonable measuring implications of the pattern of origin are still far short of attainment, the gap is probably of the Continuation type." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **another cross-pattern dependency: gap classification depends on whether a prior pattern's measured move has been achieved.**
  - **it means stop, not necessarily reverse** — value: null — "An Exhaustion Gap, taken by itself, should not be read as a sign of Reversal, or even, necessarily, of Reversal at all. It calls 'stop,' but the halt is ordinarily followed by some sort of area pattern development that may, in turn, lead to either Reversal or Continuation" (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high)
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: **none.** Neither book gives an exhaustion-gap target; E&M state that only the runaway gap carries measuring implications.
- **invalidation**: the gap not being closed within the stated window (in which case it was a runaway); the move continuing at a pace commensurate with volume.
- **detection_notes**: Computable and, unusually, well-specified. Primitives: gap detection; **gap ordinality within the move (must not be the first)**; a fill test within a 2–5 day forward window; a price-change-per-unit-volume ratio on the day after; a reversal-day test on the following bar; **and — the interesting one — a dependency on whether the originating pattern's measured move has already been reached**, which requires the gap classifier to have access to the pattern engine's target output. **That last dependency means gap classification is not a standalone primitive: breakaway / runaway / exhaustion cannot be distinguished without the pattern and trend context, and any "gap type" column computed from OHLCV alone is guessing.** This is the honest limit of a gap detector.
- **conflicts**: **On the fill window, Edwards & Magee say "most often within two to five days"; Murphy says "within a couple of days or within a week."** Overlapping but not identical; E&M's is the narrower and the more computable.

## The Island Reversal

- **origin / source_name**: Edwards & Magee, Ch. 12 → *The Island Reversal*. Murphy, Ch. 4 → *The Island Reversal*.
- **definition**: Murphy: "Sometimes after the upward exhaustion gap has formed, prices will trade in a narrow range for a couple of days or a couple of weeks before gapping to the downside. Such a situation leaves the few days of price action looking like an 'island' surrounded by space or water. The exhaus¬ tion gap to the upside followed by a breakaway gap to the down¬ side completes the island reversal pattern and usually indicates a trend reversal of some magnitude." Murphy's glossary: "A combination of an exhaustion gap in one direction and a breakaway gap in the other direction within a [short period]."
- **criteria**:
  - **structure — exhaustion gap one way, breakaway gap the other, with the island between** — value: null (fully computable) — quoted above — confidence(high)
  - **duration of the island — a couple of days to a couple of weeks (Murphy)** — "prices will trade in a narrow range for a couple of days or a couple of weeks before gapping to the downside" — confidence(high); Murphy's Fig 4.23c caption gives a concrete instance: "The second box shows a down gap **three weeks** later."
  - Murphy's Fig 4.23a caption — **within a week** — "An exhaus¬ tion gap to the upside, followed within a week by a breakaway gap to the downside, left an island reversal top." — confidence(high) — **note Murphy's own two captions say "within a week" and "three weeks later"; the duration is not fixed.**
  - **E&M downgrade its significance** — value: null — "The Island Pattern is not common and it is not, in itself, of major significance in the sense of denoting a long-term Top or Bottom, but it does, as a rule, send prices back for a complete retracement of the Minor Move that preceded it." (Ch.12) — confidence(high)
  - E&M say it is hard to trade — value: null — "An Island Pattern is not easy to trade on unless it be for a short-term 'scalp,' as, obviously, a good share of the retracement already may have been accomplished by the time the Island is charted" (Ch.12) — confidence(high)
  - islands are rare in indexes — value: null — "Exhaustion Gaps, and, in consequence, Islands, again are rare [in Averages]. The conditions that create an Exhaustion Gap seldom develop in a sufficient number of individual issues at any one time to produce a counterpart in the Averages." (E&M Ch.12) — confidence(high) — **a real universe restriction: do not expect island reversals on index charts.**
- **measured_performance**: none published.
- **price_objective**: **E&M give one and it is a retracement, not a projection**: "it does, as a rule, send prices back for a complete retracement of the **Minor** Move that preceded it." Murphy gives none, saying only that it "usually indicates a trend reversal of some magnitude."
- **invalidation**: the second gap failing to appear; the island's range being re-entered.
- **detection_notes**: **The most cleanly computable pattern in this entire file, and one of the few needing no thresholds at all.** Primitives: `gap_up[t]` then `gap_down[t+k]` for `1 <= k <= ~15`, with the constraint that `min(low[t..t+k-1]) > high[t-1]` and `min(low[t..t+k-1]) > high[t+k]` — i.e. the island's entire range sits above both the pre- and post-gap bars. Pure daily OHLCV, no volume window, no tolerance parameter, no line fitting. **The catch is upstream, not downstream: the first gap must be an EXHAUSTION gap, and per the previous section that classification is not computable without pattern and trend context.** A geometric island detector will find the shape; it cannot certify that the entering gap was exhaustion rather than runaway, which is what makes the pattern meaningful. Be explicit about that gap in any implementation.
- **conflicts**: **On significance, Edwards & Magee and Murphy disagree.** Murphy: "usually indicates a trend reversal of **some magnitude**." Edwards & Magee: "it is **not, in itself, of major significance** in the sense of denoting a long-term Top or Bottom" and expect only "a complete retracement of the **Minor** Move that preceded it." **These are materially different claims about the same pattern and they yield different targets** — Murphy implies a major reversal, E&M imply a minor-swing retracement. Record both.

## Detection primitives required, by frequency of use

| Primitive | Patterns needing it | Computable from daily OHLCV? |
|---|---|---|

| Touch counting **with departure** (approach + bounce) | trendline validity, all boundary lines | Yes |
| Neckline as a **2-point sloping line**, extrapolated forward | H&S top/bottom/complex, diamond | Yes |
| Percentage penetration on **closes** (3% / 2% / 1%) | everything | Yes — but pick and record the denominator |
| Consecutive-close counter (2-day rule); weekday test (Friday rule) | everything | Yes |
| Apex intersection + `elapsed / base-to-apex` ratio | symmetrical, ascending, descending, wedge | Yes |
| Gap detection (`low[t] > high[t-1]`) | all gaps, island | Yes |
| Volume ratio vs a window | almost every pattern | **Window and threshold are NOT published — the number is yours** |
| Volume trend (monotone decline / expansion) | triangles, flags, pennants, wedges, broadening, rectangles | Yes, but "appreciably and constantly" is unquantified |
| Per-leg volume comparison (up-legs vs down-legs) | ascending/descending triangle, rectangle continuation-vs-reversal | Yes — needs no external window, under-used |
| Price-change per unit volume | runaway vs exhaustion gap | Yes |
| **Curvature fit (quadratic/spline) on price AND on volume** | rounding tops/bottoms, scallops | Yes, but not a pivot method |
| **Half-vs-half shape similarity (pivot count, spacing, amplitude)** | complex/multiple H&S, H&S symmetry, diamond | Yes — mandatory, and the main gap in template-based detectors |
| Dwell/curvature at a pivot ("rounded" vs "spiky") | double bottom vs double top discrimination | Yes |
| **Log-price transform of the whole series** | falling wedge (mandatory), flag/pennant targets, runaway-gap targets | Yes — and E&M say arithmetic scaling *manufactures* falling wedges |
| Weekly / monthly resampling | rounding bottoms, scallops, weekly reversals, long triangles, saucer detection | Yes |
| Prior-breakout event as an anchor | flag/pennant measured move, runaway-gap measured move | Yes — **cross-pattern dependency**, not a swing low |
| Prior pattern's target-attainment state | runaway vs exhaustion gap classification | Yes — **cross-pattern dependency** |
| Concurrent index/breadth series | E&M's descending-triangle throwback rule | **NO — cross-instrument, not per-symbol OHLCV** |
| Rendered-chart geometry (45° slope, "nearly square", "prominence") | Murphy's trendline angle, E&M's rectangle aspect, spike prominence | **NO — scale/rendering dependent, not computable as stated** |
| Intraday ticks | E&M note most breakaway gaps are intraday and invisible on daily bars | **NO — daily bars structurally under-count** |
