# Failure Modes and Base Rates for Multi-Bar Chart Patterns and Bases

**Scope.** How multi-week chart patterns and bases FAIL, what published numbers exist for that failure, and — the discipline this file exists to enforce — whether any of those numbers were published next to the unconditional base rate that would make them interpretable. Single-candlestick patterns are out of scope.

**A note on what "failure rate" means.** It means at least five incompatible things in the literature below. Bulkowski's "break even failure rate" is *fails to move 5% in the breakout direction* — it is not "the trade lost money". Bulkowski's "busted" is *moved less than 10% then crossed to the other side*. Chang/Osler/Savin's "failure" is *the pattern did not predict the sign of an excess return*. Lo-Mamaysky-Wang never use the word failure at all; they ask whether the conditional return distribution differs from the unconditional one. IBD's "failure" is a discretionary 7-8% stop. **These numbers cannot be compared to each other and must never be averaged.**

## 1. False breakout / break-even failure (Bulkowski's 5% definition)

- **source(s)**: Thomas Bulkowski, thepatternsite.com Glossary; individual pattern pages (hst.html, st.html, at.html); *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* 2nd/3rd ed. is where these are collected. The site is the live primary; the book restates it.
- **definition**: Verbatim, break even failure rate is *"A measure of the number of patterns that fail to rise (upward breakout) or decline (downward breakout) by at least 5%."* The glossary's `failure rate` entry is literally *"See break even failure rate"* — there is no second definition. Critically: **there is no time limit in the definition and no reference to the pivot being lost.** A pattern that breaks out, rises 4%, then collapses 40% counts as a "failure"; a pattern that rises 6% then collapses 40% does **not**. The measurement runs breakout → "ultimate high"/"ultimate low", not breakout → fixed horizon. Bulkowski also applies "5% failure rate" to *trades*, not just patterns — on BestEntry.html the same phrase measures entry methods.
- **published_rates**:
  - Head-and-shoulders top, break-even failure rate — 19% — "more than 2,800 perfect trades", statistics updated 8/26/2020, no date range given for the underlying price data — "Break even failure rate: 19%" / "The above numbers are based on more than 2,800 perfect trades." — confidence(med — the number is verbatim; the sample is described only as "perfect trades", see failure mode 17)
  - Head-and-shoulders top, average decline — 16% — same sample — "Average decline: 16%" — confidence(med)
  - Head-and-shoulders top, pullback rate — 68% — same sample — "Pullback rate: 68%" — confidence(med)
  - Symmetrical triangle, break-even failure rate up/down — 25% / 37% — "over 3,000 perfect trades", no date range — "Break even failure rate for up/down breakouts: 25%/37%" — confidence(med)
  - Symmetrical triangle, percentage meeting price target up/down — 58% / 36% — same — "Percentage meeting price target for up/down breakouts: 58%/36%" — confidence(med)
  - Symmetrical triangle, throwback/pullback rate — 62% / 65% — same — "Throwback/pullback rate: 62%/65%" — confidence(med)
  - Ascending triangle, break-even failure rate up/down — 17% / 38% — "more than 1,400 perfect trades" — "17%/38%" — confidence(med)
  - Ascending triangle, percentage meeting price target up/down — 70% / 44% — same — "70%/44%" — confidence(med)
  - Ascending triangle, breakout direction — upward 63% of the time — same — "Upward 63% of the time and 64% of the way to the triangle apex (for both breakout directions)" — confidence(med)
  - Double bottom, 5% failure rate when buying "as soon as possible" — 37.4% — 502 stocks, January 1990 to December 2018, bull-market data only, "more than 2,200" trades — "In these tests, I used only bull market data from January 1990 to December 2018 on 502 stocks." + "the median is 10% and the 5% failure rate is 37.4%." — confidence(high)
  - Double bottom, 5% failure rate when waiting for confirmation — 17.9% — same test — "the median was 22% and the 5% failure rate was 17.9%." — confidence(high)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — the rate is published without a benchmark.** Not one of these pages states what fraction of randomly-selected stocks in the same universe over the same period rose 5% from a random date, which is the only thing that would make a 19% or 25% "failure rate" mean anything. The BestEntry test comes closest — it holds the pattern constant and varies the *entry rule*, which is an internally-controlled comparison — but it still supplies no unconditional benchmark. Note also that BestEntry restricts to "only bull market data", which mechanically depresses the failure rate of a long-side pattern relative to any all-weather baseline.
- **detection_notes**: From daily OHLCV this is directly computable — record the breakout close and the pivot, then track the running maximum favourable excursion. But the definition is *unbounded in time*: a pattern is not a "break-even failure" until you know its ultimate high, which you only know in hindsight. **A live detector cannot compute Bulkowski's failure rate on a live pattern.** The only honest live states are `triggered` and `not yet 5%`. Never render a live detection with the historical break-even failure rate attached as if it were the probability of this instance failing.
- **conflicts**: See failure mode 17 — every number above is qualified as "perfect trades"/"perfect patterns", and Savin/Weller/Zvingelis measured that tightening a detector toward Bulkowski's own visual criteria changed the population by 30% while changing predictive power not at all.

## 2. Busted patterns — single, double, triple bust

- **source(s)**: Bulkowski, Busted.html, BustPerformance.html, BustDoubleTops.html, BustTripleTops.html, BustHST.html; *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* 3rd ed.
- **definition**: Verbatim: *"A busted chart pattern is one in which price breaks out in one direction from a chart pattern, but moves no more than 10% before reversing and breaking out in the new direction."* Four required elements, verbatim: (1) *"Price breaks out in the normal manner with price closing below the bottom (downward breakout), above the top (upward breakout)"*, (2) *"Price continues no more than 10% in the breakout direction."*, (3) *"Price reverses direction."*, (4) *"Price closes on the side opposite the breakout direction."* The glossary phrases the same thing as *"Price that moves no more than 10% after the breakout before reversing and zipping to the other side of the chart pattern, starting a trend in a new direction."* Multiple busts: *"A double bust for the double top means price busts the downward breakout as described above, moves higher by no more than 10% before reversing."* — i.e. a double bust is a bust that itself busts. **Bust is therefore a strictly stronger condition than break-even failure**: it requires the price to cross the whole pattern, not merely to under-perform 5%.
- **published_rates**:
  - Double tops that bust — 36% — sample size and date range NOT stated; sourced to *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* 3rd ed., page updated 12/28/2020 — "36% of double tops will bust" — confidence(med)
  - Of busted double tops: single / double / triple+ — 67% / 5% / 28% — same, no n — "67% will single bust", "5% will double bust", "28% of them will triple bust" — confidence(med)
  - Single-busted double top, subsequent rise — 54% average — same, no n — "Single busted patterns will see price rise by 54% on average" — confidence(med)
  - Non-busted comparison for double tops — 50% average rise — same, no n — "Non-busted double tops (which is a double bottom) will see price rise by 50% on average" — confidence(med)
  - All busted double tops, move in the busted direction — 38% average — same, no n — "The following move in the busted direction (upward) averages 38%" — confidence(med)
  - Triple tops that bust — 43% — no n, no date range — "43% of triple tops will bust" — confidence(med)
  - Of busted triple tops: single / double / triple+ — 67% / 3% / 31% — no n — "67% will single bust", "3% will double bust", "31% of them will triple bust" — confidence(med)
  - Single-busted triple top, subsequent rise — 60% average — no n — "Single busted patterns will see price rise by 60% on average" — confidence(med)
  - Non-busted triple top comparison — 46% average rise — no n — "Non-busted triple tops (which is to say, triple bottoms) see price rise by an average of 46%" — confidence(med)
  - Busted triple tops within 100 days of a trend start vs beyond — 37% vs 23% — 92 samples vs 115 samples — reported on BustTripleTops.html as the trend-start finding; I have the numbers and the two sample sizes but not the sentence verbatim — confidence(low — numbers read from the page's summary, not quoted)
  - Head-and-shoulders tops that bust — 32% — no n — "32% of head-and-shoulders will bust" — confidence(med)
  - Of busted H&S tops: single / double / triple+ — 67% / 3% / 30% — no n — "67% will single bust", "3% will double bust", "30% of them will triple bust" — confidence(med)
  - Single-busted H&S top, subsequent rise — 67% average — no n — "Single busted patterns will see price rise by 67% on average" — confidence(med)
  - Non-busted H&S comparison — 45% average rise — no n — "Non-busted head-and-shoulders will see price rise by 45% on average" — confidence(med)
  - Cross-pattern medians (BustPerformance.html, 30+ patterns, statistics "updated on 12/29/2020") — 24% of upward breakouts bust, 40% of downward breakouts bust; of busts, single 53% up / 67% down, double 31% up / 4% down, triple+ 16% up / 28% down — no n, no date range — confidence(low — read from a table by the fetch summariser, not quoted verbatim; treat as a pointer, re-read the page before using)
- **base_rate_stated**: **Partially — and this is the one place Bulkowski does supply a comparison.** For each busted pattern he prints the busted move *against the non-busted move of the mirror pattern* (busted double top 54% vs "non-busted double tops (which is a double bottom) will see price rise by 50%"). That is a **within-pattern** benchmark, not an unconditional one. He still never says what a randomly chosen stock in the same database did over the same window, so the reader cannot tell whether 54% is skill or drift. Answer for the field: **NO unconditional base rate is given; a mirror-pattern comparison is.**
- **detection_notes**: This is the single most computable failure state in the whole file, and the most important one for a detector. From daily OHLCV: on breakout, latch the breakout price and the pattern's opposite boundary. Track max excursion. If max excursion in the breakout direction never exceeds 10% AND a later close prints on the opposite side of the pattern, transition the detection to `busted`. Because the 10% cap is checked *before* the reversal, a detector can evaluate it causally with no look-ahead: at every bar you already know the running excursion. **A detector that keeps publishing "head-and-shoulders top, target X" after price has closed back above the head is publishing a state the source itself says has flipped direction.** Bust detection is also the natural expiry mechanism: a triggered pattern that busts is finished, not pending.
- **conflicts**: **Busted.html and BustTripleTops.html disagree for triple tops, and I record both without reconciling them.** Busted.html: *"single busts happen 19% of the time. Double and triple busts occur 1% of the time, each."* BustTripleTops.html: *"43% of triple tops will bust"* of which *"67% will single bust"* (≈29% of all triple tops single-busting) and *"3% will double bust"*, *"31% of them will triple bust"*. 19% ≠ 29% and 1% ≠ 1.3%/13.3%. The pages may use different denominators, different editions of the book, or different update dates (12/28/2020 vs undated). **Neither page states its sample size, so the discrepancy cannot be adjudicated from the published material.** Do not average them; do not pick one.

## 3. Failure rates are not stationary — patterns fail more than they used to

- **source(s)**: Bulkowski's Failure Rate Study (FailureRates.html) and Time Performance (TimePerformance.html).
- **definition**: Here "failure" is the **10%** variant, not the 5% one — the percentage of patterns failing to move 10% in the breakout direction. Bulkowski runs it as a time series by breakout year.
- **published_rates**:
  - Study scale — "nearly 14,000" patterns (elsewhere on the page, 13,932 across 23 pattern types) — 1991 to 2008 — "A study using nearly 14,000 chart patterns covering the years from 1991 to 2008" — confidence(high for the quoted sentence; med for the 13,932/23 breakdown, which I have as a number but not as a verbatim sentence)
  - 10% failure rate, first and peak year — 11% in 1991 → 44% in 2007 — same sample — "In 1991, the 10% failure rate stood at 11%. By 2007, the failure rate peaked at 44%" — confidence(high)
  - Overall conclusion — two to four times more failures — same sample — "chart patterns fail between two and four times more often now than they did in the past" — confidence(high)
  - Upward breakouts, 10% failure rate, 1990s vs 2003-2007 — roughly 14% → 28% — same sample — numbers read from the page's summary; the individual figures 14% and 28% were quoted but I do not have the full sentence — confidence(low)
  - Downward breakouts, 10% failure rate, 1990s vs 2000s — 26% → 49% — same sample — same caveat — confidence(low)
  - Decade drift, example pattern (bump-and-run reversal bottom average rise) — 44% in the 1990s, 67% in the 2000s, 65% in the 2010s — TimePerformance.html compares "56 patterns" on up breakouts and "53 patterns" on down breakouts, statistics updated through 25 Aug 2020 — confidence(low — read from a table, direction of drift is not monotone across patterns)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — the rate is published without a benchmark.** The study never reports what fraction of *all* stocks in the database moved 10% in a given year. The 1991→2007 rise in "failure" is therefore inseparable from the fall in cross-sectional volatility and from whatever the market did in each year. This is the clearest case in the file where a base rate would have changed the interpretation completely: a market where fewer stocks move 10% will show a rising "failure rate" for every pattern regardless of whether the patterns got worse.
- **detection_notes**: For a detector this is a warning against baking any constant into the product. If a published failure rate can double in fifteen years within one author's own database, then a hard-coded "19% failure rate" label on a UI is a claim about 1991-2008 presented as a claim about today. Any rate shown must be recomputed on the same universe and window the detector actually runs on, or not shown.
- **conflicts**: None internal, but note this study uses the **10%** threshold while the per-pattern pages use **5%** — the same word "failure rate" carries two different numbers on the same website.

## 4. Premature and unconfirmed breakouts (the pattern that never becomes a pattern)

- **source(s)**: Bulkowski on the Best Entry (BestEntry.html).
- **definition**: A pattern "confirms" when price closes beyond the pattern boundary (for a double bottom, above the highest high between the two bottoms). A *premature* signal is a buy trigger that fires before confirmation. An *unconfirmed* pattern is one that looked like a double bottom and never confirmed at all — in Bulkowski's framing it is not a failed pattern, it is **not a pattern**.
- **published_rates**:
  - Fraction of double bottoms that confirmed — 52% — 502 stocks, January 1990-December 2018, bull-market data only — "52% of patterns confirmed" — confidence(med — quoted as a fragment, not a full sentence)
  - Of confirmed patterns, fraction that had already flashed a buy trigger before confirmation — 41% — same sample — "of the patterns which later confirmed, 41% showed a buy signal trigger before confirmation." — confidence(high)
  - Outcome of unconfirmed double bottoms — 48% closed below the second bottom — same sample — "Forty-eight percent of unconfirmed double bottoms close below the bottom of the second bottom, resulting in a loss." — confidence(high)
  - Trades where the buy stop triggered but the pattern never confirmed — 6% — same sample — "Only 6% of the trades did not confirm, meaning the buy stop triggered but the pattern never confirmed." — confidence(med)
  - Test scale — "more than 2,200" trades in the first test; 5,467 trades across the full test table — January 1990-December 2018, 502 stocks — "The number of trades were more than 2,200, so I had plenty of samples." — confidence(high for the quoted sentence; med for 5,467, read from the page rather than quoted)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — the rate is published without a benchmark.** There is no statement of how often a randomly selected stock in the same 502-name, 1990-2018 bull-market sample fell below an arbitrary prior swing low. The 48% figure sounds damning and may be entirely ordinary.
- **detection_notes**: This is the failure mode most likely to be mishandled in software, because it happens *before* the trade. A shape can be geometrically complete and never confirm. The detector must therefore maintain `forming` as a state distinct from `triggered`, and must not count `forming` detections in any hit-rate it publishes — 48% of the unconfirmed ones go on to break the low. The 41% figure is the operational warning: on nearly half of the *eventual* winners, an early-entry rule fires first, which means any evaluation that permits early entry is measuring a different population from one that requires a confirming close. Pick one rule, state it, and never mix populations. From daily OHLCV, confirmation is a *close* beyond the boundary, not an intraday touch — using the high instead of the close silently converts this into an easier, more optimistic test.
- **conflicts**: Bulkowski's own three entry rules produce 5%-failure rates of 37.4%, 17.9% and (best of table, number not quoted) for the same underlying patterns. **The entry rule moves the "failure rate" by a factor of two on identical pattern instances.** Any single published failure rate for a named pattern is therefore under-specified unless the entry rule is stated with it.

## 5. Throwback and pullback as failure precursors

- **source(s)**: Bulkowski Glossary; Study of Studies (studystudy.html); per-pattern pages.
- **definition**: Verbatim — throwback: *"Price breaks out upward, climbs, and then returns to the breakout price within 30 days."* Pullback: *"Price breaks out downward, drops, and then returns to the breakout price within 30 days."* Note the hard **30-day window** — this is the only failure-adjacent construct in Bulkowski with an explicit time bound, and it is a useful precedent for detector expiry.
- **published_rates**:
  - Share of chart pattern types performing better without a throwback (upward breakouts) — 97% — across the pattern types in his database; per-type n not stated on this page — "Yes: 97% of the time chart patterns with upward breakouts perform better post breakout without a throwback" — confidence(med)
  - Share of chart pattern types performing better without a pullback (downward breakouts) — 91% — same — "Yes: 91% of chart pattern types with downward breakouts perform better if a pullback does NOT occur" — confidence(med)
  - Head-and-shoulders top pullback rate — 68% — "more than 2,800 perfect trades" — "Pullback rate: 68%" — confidence(med)
  - Symmetrical triangle throwback/pullback rate — 62%/65% — "over 3,000 perfect trades" — "Throwback/pullback rate: 62%/65%" — confidence(med)
  - Ascending triangle throwback/pullback rate — 64%/63% — "more than 1,400 perfect trades" — "64%/63%" — confidence(med)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — the rate is published without a benchmark.** Notice how strange the missing benchmark is here: a throwback rate near 65% for every pattern type suggests the number may be describing mean-reversion in the underlying stocks, not anything about the pattern. Without the unconditional probability that a stock which just closed 3% above a 40-day high returns to that level within 30 days, a 64% throwback rate is uninterpretable. **The uniformity of the throwback rates across unrelated patterns (62%, 63%, 64%, 65%, 68%) is itself evidence that the number is a property of stocks, not of patterns.**
- **detection_notes**: A throwback is *not* a failure — the "97% of pattern types perform better without one" statement is about degree, not direction. A detector should record `threw_back: true/false` as an attribute of a triggered detection and must not flip the state to `failed` on a throwback alone. The state that matters is the bust (mode 2) or the loss of the stop (mode 7). Concretely, from daily bars: after an up breakout at price P, `threw_back` is true if any close within 30 calendar days trades back to ≤ P after first exceeding it.
- **conflicts**: A secondary source in search results asserted both *"patterns with throwbacks actually perform worse overall"* and *"if a throwback completes and price resumes its original breakout direction, the eventual move is often stronger"*. I could not attribute the second claim to a primary source and do not treat it as established.

## 6. High-volume breakout failure (the finding that contradicts every textbook)

- **source(s)**: Bulkowski, New Volume Study (VolumeStudy.html).
- **definition**: Compares breakout-day volume to the preceding 31 calendar days, then measures the post-breakout move and the failure rate for above- vs below-average breakout volume.
- **published_rates**:
  - Failure rate, upward breakouts, above- vs below-average breakout volume — 14% vs 5% — "over 8,000 chart patterns (samples) from July 1991 to May 2011", restricted to rectangles and double/triple tops and bottoms — "I looked over 8,000 chart patterns (samples) from July 1991 to May 2011, but included only rectangles, double and triple tops and bottoms." + "Failures occur more often after a high volume breakout. The differences are significant, 14% versus 5% for upward breakouts on above/below average volume, respectively." — confidence(high)
  - General statement of the effect size — at least 2x — same sample — "Chart patterns with above average breakout volume are at least twice as likely to fail than are those with below average breakout volume." — confidence(high)
  - Performance benefit of high-volume breakout — 1.8 percentage points — same sample — "An upward breakout on above average volume propels the stock higher by just 1.8 percentage points" — confidence(med)
  - Throwback/pullback likelihood after a high-volume breakout — 3x — same sample — "Price is three times as likely to throwback or pullback after an above average volume breakout." — confidence(med)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — the rate is published without a benchmark**, but this study is structurally better than the others in this file because it is *internally controlled*: the same patterns, the same period, split by one variable. The 5% arm functions as a control for the 14% arm. It still lacks an unconditional stock-level base rate.
- **detection_notes**: Directly implementable from daily OHLCV — breakout-day volume ÷ mean volume over the trailing 31 calendar days. The implication for product is uncomfortable: **a "confirmed by heavy volume" badge, which nearly every screener ships, is associated with a 14% rather than 5% failure rate in the one study that measured it on 8,000 patterns.** A detector should not display volume confirmation as a quality upgrade unless it has measured that on its own universe. Note the study covers rectangles and double/triple tops and bottoms only — it does not license the claim for cup-with-handle or flags.
- **conflicts**: **This directly contradicts Bulkowski's own Study of Studies**, which reports for breakout volume *"Upward breakouts: Heavy: 79% (above average breakout volume helps), Light: 21%"* — i.e. 79% of pattern types perform better on heavy breakout volume. Both numbers are Bulkowski's. They measure different things (share of *pattern types* whose average move is larger, vs the *failure rate* within one restricted sample) and the reconciliation is that heavy volume raises the mean move slightly (1.8pp) while roughly tripling the failure rate — a fatter, more bimodal distribution. **Record both. Do not average. Do not present either as "volume confirms breakouts".**

## 7. Undercut-and-rally / shakeout vs. genuine breakdown

- **source(s)**: O'Neil/IBD tradition, via secondary sources only — thweis.com FAQ; TraderHR and Freedom Trades summaries surfaced in search; IBD's own material was unreachable (investors.com blocked). Minervini's treatment via a tertiary flashcard deck.
- **definition**: A shakeout is a **failed breakdown**: price closes below a defined support (base low, prior pivot, handle low), triggers stops, and reclaims that level, ideally on expanding volume. Secondary phrasings, verbatim: *"a failed breakdown. It is a classic shakeout where price dips below a major key level, shakes out the weak holders by hitting their stop losses, and then snaps back above"*; *"when a stock price falls below a key support level (undercut), it triggers stop-loss orders and causes panic selling. However, instead of continued downward movement, the stock price quickly reverses"*. The discriminating criterion in the sources is **the close, plus volume**: verbatim from thweis, *"When a stock is able to absorb swift selling by closing strong the same day on high volume, it can be considered support!"* versus *"A sell-off closing weak (under PLL or short term MA support) on high volume is a red flag and probably not a shakeout."* The same source explicitly refuses a numeric rule: analysis must be *"relative to the history of the stock at hand"*.
- **published_rates**:
  - Rate at which an undercut of a base low resolves as a shakeout rather than a breakdown — **value: null** — `missing:` **no source I reached publishes any rate at all for this.** Not IBD, not O'Neil, not Minervini, not Bulkowski. The entire construct is asserted, illustrated with examples, and never measured. This is the largest evidential hole in this file.
  - Bar-count window for the shakeout to resolve — **value: null** — `missing:` secondary sources mention "1-3 days" for a shakeout zone and "4-8%" risk from entry, but neither figure is attributed to a primary source or to a measured sample. I do not record them as findings.
  - O'Neil's 7-8% stop, cited as the discipline that overrides the shakeout judgement — 7-8% — no sample, it is a rule not a measurement — "cut all losses at 7-8% without exception" (secondary paraphrase of O'Neil) — confidence(low)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — and worse, there is no rate at all to attach one to.** Nobody in this tradition has published the unconditional probability that a stock which closes below a 30-day low reclaims it within N days. That unconditional number is trivially computable from daily bars and would immediately tell you whether "undercut and rally" identifies anything.
- **detection_notes**: Fully computable from daily OHLCV and **this is where a detector can add real value rather than restating folklore**: (a) mark the base low L; (b) flag `undercut` when a daily close < L; (c) flag `reclaimed` when a subsequent close > L within a stated window; (d) publish the empirical reclaim rate *for your universe*, alongside the unconditional reclaim rate for any close below any 30-day low in the same universe. If the two are equal, "undercut and rally" is not a pattern, it is a description of noise. Crucially, the detector must not retroactively relabel: a detection that went `undercut` and then `reclaimed` must retain both events in its history, or the UI will show only successful shakeouts and the failed undercuts will vanish — which is exactly how this folklore stays alive.
- **conflicts**: None recorded, because there are no competing numbers — only competing prose.

## 8. Base failure by base count / stage

- **source(s)**: William O'Neil via IBD's syndicated "Investor's Corner" (secondary — Yahoo Finance syndication of IBD); Mark Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard* (tertiary — flashcard deck).
- **definition**: Bases are counted from the start of a new advance. IBD's counting rule, verbatim from the syndicated article: *"A breakout needs to produce a gain of at least 20% in order to be counted as one stage."* If the gain falls short, verbatim, *"it's a base-on-base pattern and counted as one stage."* Failure here means the breakout from a third-or-later-stage base does not produce a sustained advance and triggers the sell discipline.
- **published_rates**:
  - Failure rate of third- or fourth-stage bases — **value: null** — `missing:` **IBD and O'Neil publish the rule without any measured rate.** The strongest statement I found is an assertion, verbatim from O'Neil as quoted by IBD: *"Sell when your stock makes a new high in price off a third- or fourth-stage base. The third time is seldom a charm in the market. By then, an advancing stock has become too obvious, and almost everyone sees it."* That is a rationale, not a measurement. No sample, no period, no count.
  - Minervini on late base counts — **value: null** — `missing:` the flashcard deck renders his position as *"Base 4 and 5 fail more frequently."* — a comparative claim with no number, no sample and no period. And this is a tertiary source; I did not read the book.
  - Illustrative single case (Keurig Green Mountain, fifth-stage base) — n=1 — "reversed to trigger the 8% sell rule" and "plunged 71% by Nov. 11." — confidence(low — a single anecdote, recorded only to show that the published support for this rule is anecdotal)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — and there is no rate to benchmark.** Neither house publishes the failure rate of a *first*-stage base either, so even the internal comparison ("third-stage bases fail more than first-stage") is unquantified in the published material. The claim is universally repeated and, as far as the reachable sources go, never measured.
- **detection_notes**: Base counting is mechanically implementable from daily OHLCV — track advances from a prior base breakout and reset the count when the ≥20% threshold is met, treat sub-20% advances as base-on-base. But **the detector must not display a stage number as a risk signal**, because the risk claim attached to the stage number has no published support. If the product wants to use base count, it must measure the outcome by stage on its own universe first. This is a case where an honest detector's correct output is a *measured* number of its own or nothing at all. Note also that the 20% threshold makes the stage count itself path-dependent and revisable: a base that has advanced 18% is not yet a completed stage, so the stage number of a live detection can change under it. That is a repainting hazard (mode 15) hiding inside a taxonomy.
- **conflicts**: None — there is nothing to conflict, which is the finding.

## 9. Wide-and-loose bases, late-stage bases, climax tops

- **source(s)**: Minervini, *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard* (tertiary — flashcard deck); IBD/O'Neil via syndication.
- **definition**: A constructive base contracts; a faulty base widens. Verbatim from the Minervini cards: *"Pattern should not get wider (up and down movements); up is good but wild swings back and forth are not."* The constructive alternative, verbatim: *"A common characteristic of virtually all constructive price structures (those under accumulation) is a contraction of volatility accompanied by specific areas in the base structure where volume contracts significantly."* A climax top is the late-stage acceleration that ends an advance; the criteria offered in the tradition (exhaustion gaps, largest weekly gain of the move, widest daily spread) were not obtainable from a primary source I could reach.
- **published_rates**:
  - Number of volatility contractions in a valid VCP — two to six — no sample, no period; this is a design rule, not a measurement — "During a VCP, you'll generally see a succession of anywhere from two to six contractions." — confidence(low — tertiary source; the criterion is asserted, not measured)
  - Depth of a constructive correction — 10% to 35% — no sample — "Most constructive setups correct between 10% and 35%." — confidence(low — tertiary; "most" is unquantified)
  - Depth threshold treated as disqualifying — 60% — no sample — "Mark rarely buys a stock that has corrected 60% or more; a stock that is down that much often signals a serious problem." — confidence(low — tertiary; a personal rule)
  - Base duration range — 3 to 45 weeks, most 7-25 — no sample — "Can form in as few as 3 weeks to as many as 45 weeks (most are 7-25 weeks)" — confidence(low — tertiary)
  - Measured failure rate of a wide-and-loose base versus a tight one — **value: null** — `missing:` **no source I reached publishes one.** The criteria are stated as diagnostic; the diagnosis has never been scored.
  - Measured failure rate following a climax top — **value: null** — `missing:` same. IBD's climax-top material was behind investors.com, which the fetcher cannot reach; nothing in the reachable secondary material carries a measured rate.
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — there are no rates, so no benchmarks.** Note that the depth criteria (10-35% constructive, 60% disqualifying) are stated without the distribution of base depths in the universe, so there is no way to know whether "10-35%" describes a selective filter or simply describes most bases.
- **detection_notes**: All of these are computable — base depth as (base high − base low)/base high; "widening" as a monotonicity test on successive pullback depths within the base; contraction count as the number of successive lower-amplitude pullbacks; tightness as a ratio of recent to earlier weekly ranges. **But computing them is not the same as validating them**, and a detector that filters on 10-35% depth and 2-6 contractions is applying an unvalidated screen, which will look like precision to a user. The honest presentation is to publish the measured criteria as *attributes* of a detection ("depth 23%, 3 contractions") rather than as *quality scores*, unless the product has measured the outcome split on its own data.
- **conflicts**: None recorded; the numbers in this section come from a single tradition and a single (tertiary) rendering of it.

## 10. Market-condition dependence — follow-through days

- **source(s)**: Rob Hanna, Quantifiable Edges, *IBD Follow Through Days pt. 1 — Are they predictive?* (2008) — **a blog, secondary, but it is an independent replication attempt of an IBD claim and reports its method and counts**; IBD's own claim as quoted therein.
- **definition**: The FTD is IBD's market-level confirmation signal. Verbatim from the replication: *"The first up day from a bottom in the major indexes is Day 1 of an attempted rally"*, and an FTD occurs when *"on Day 4 or later, the market closes up sharply on higher volume than the previous session"*, operationalised as *"a rise of at least 1% in one of the major averages accompanied by an increase in volume over the previous day"* (also tested at 1.7%). The trigger population was defined by an 8% S&P 500 decline, chosen because 10% *"seemed too large"* and a smaller threshold caught *"every minor correction"*.
- **published_rates**:
  - FTD success rate at the 1% threshold — 54.7% — 35 of 64 FTDs, "37 years of data" from December 1971 to 11 January 2008, S&P 500 — "35 of 64 possible Follow Through Days were successful for a success rate of 54.7%" — confidence(med — blog, but the counts are stated)
  - FTD success rate at the 1.7% threshold — 55.7% — 29 of 52 FTDs, same period — "29 of 52 possible Follow Through Days being labeled 'successful'. This equals a 55.7% success rate" — confidence(med)
  - IBD's own claimed success rate — 70%-80% — sample and period NOT stated by IBD as quoted — "Follow Through Days carry a success rate of between 70%-80%." — confidence(low — this is IBD's claim as reported by a third party; I could not reach IBD's own statement of it)
  - Replication verdict — *"I was unable to duplicate IBD's success rate...even though I made the definitions of 'success' and 'failure' as liberal as I could."* — confidence(med)
- **base_rate_stated**: **NO — the rate is published without a benchmark**, and here the absence is fatal to interpretation. A 54.7% "success rate" for a signal that fires after an 8% decline must be read against the unconditional probability that the market rallies from *any* point after an 8% decline. Since equities drift up, that unconditional number is plausibly close to 54.7%, and nothing in the study rules it out. **The study measures the signal without measuring the drift the signal is riding.**
- **detection_notes**: Fully computable from index daily OHLCV plus volume. The relevance to a pattern detector is conditioning: if breakout success is market-conditional, then a per-pattern failure rate computed across all market regimes is a mixture, and quoting it for today's regime is a category error. The practical requirement is that a detector record the market state (index above/below a long MA, distance from index high, FTD/distribution state) as a field on every detection, so that outcomes can later be split by regime rather than pooled.
- **conflicts**: **IBD claims 70-80%; the independent replication got 54.7% and 55.7% over 64 and 52 signals respectively.** Record both. The gap is ~20 percentage points and neither party publishes a base rate, so neither number is interpretable on its own.

- **definition**: Weinstein's four stages, verbatim from the secondary rendering: Stage 1 *"Basing Phase. May begin accumulation."*; Stage 2 *"Advancing Stage."*; Stage 3 *"The Top Area. Start to reduce positions."*; Stage 4 *"The Declining Stage. Avoid on the long side."* The doctrine is that breakouts are only to be taken in Stage 2 and only when the general market is not in Stage 4.
- **published_rates**:
  - Weinstein's breakout success rate conditional on market stage — **value: null** — `missing:` **the reachable Weinstein material contains no statistics at all.** The stage framework is entirely qualitative; the page I fetched does not even state the 30-week MA rule, and I found no published measurement of Stage-2-only breakout performance versus unfiltered.

  - LMW, patterns significant on Nasdaq by goodness-of-fit — 10 of 10 — same design, Nasdaq sample, 1962-1996 — "there is overwhelming significance for all ten indicators in the Nasdaq sample, with p-values that are zero to three significant digits, and test statistics Q that range from 34.12 to 92.09." — confidence(high)

However, for the other five patterns, the p-values range from 0.104 for IRS [IHS] to 0.393 for DBOT" — confidence(high; the OCR renders IHS as "IRS")

- **detection_notes**: The LMW result is the strongest published evidence that pattern informativeness is *venue- and population-conditional*: the same ten detectors, the same algorithm, the same 34-year window, and the answer flips between NYSE/AMEX and Nasdaq — and between two goodness-of-fit tests on the same NYSE/AMEX data (7 of 10 by Q, 5 of 10 by K-S). For a detector this means a pattern validated on large caps has not been validated on small caps, and a statistic computed under one test is not the statistic under another. Store the universe and the test with every published rate.
- **conflicts**: LMW's own two tests disagree on the same data — 7 of 10 significant by the decile goodness-of-fit statistic, 5 of 10 by Kolmogorov-Smirnov. Record both. The authors note the sampling distributions of both statistics assume i.i.d. returns, *"which is not plausible for financial data"*.

## 12. Data snooping — the published edge does not survive out of sample

- **source(s)**: Sullivan, Timmermann & White, FMG DP303 / *Journal of Finance* 54(5), 1999. Park & Irwin, *Journal of Economic Surveys* 21(4), 2007.
- **definition**: Data snooping is the inflation of apparent significance caused by selecting the best rule from a large universe searched over the same data. STW's remedy is White's Reality Check bootstrap, which prices the *whole search*, not the winner. "Failure" here is a rule whose apparent edge disappears once the search is priced in, or once it is run forward.
- **published_rates**:
  - Size of the searched universe — 7,846 trading rules — "We consider a very large number (7,846) of trading rules drawn from a wide variety of rule specifications." — confidence(high)
  - Data — 100 years of daily DJIA, 1897-1996, plus S&P 500 futures 1984-1996 — "apply the rules to 100 years of daily data on the Dow Jones Industrial Average" — confidence(high)
  - Best rule in-sample, full universe, 1897-1996 — 17.17% mean annualised return, 6,310 trades, break-even transaction cost 0.27% per trade — "the best-performing trading rule for the Dow Jones Industrial Average earned a mean annualized return of 17.17 percent resulting from 6,310 trades (63.1 per year), giving a break-even transaction cost level of 0.27 percent per trade." — confidence(high)
  - Same rule, genuine out-of-sample 1987-1996 — 2.8% mean return, **nominal** p-value 0.322 — 10 years, DJIA — "The five-day moving average rule selected from the full universe produces a mean return of 2.8 percent with a nominal p-value of 0.322 for the period 1987 to 1996, indicating that the best trading rule, as of the end of 1986, did not continue to generate valuable economic signals in the subsequent ten-year period." — confidence(high)
  - Out-of-sample, best rule in isolation vs. jointly — p = 0.05 in isolation; Reality Check p ends around 0.15 — 26 BLL rules, 1987-1996 — "The p-value for the best-performing trading rule, considered in isolation, is 0.05." and "The p-value starts out around 0.3, decreases to around 0.13, but then slowly increases to 0.15." — confidence(high)
  - Overall verdict — "we also find that the superior performance of the best technical trading rule is not repeated in the out-of-sample experiment covering the ten-year period 1987–1996. In this sample the results are completely reversed and the best performing trading rule is not even statistically significant at standard critical levels." — confidence(high)
  - Park & Irwin survey tally — 56 positive / 20 negative / 19 mixed of 95 modern studies — literature survey, "early" and "modern" studies — "Among a total of 95 modern studies, 56 studies find positive results regarding technical trading strategies, 20 studies obtain negative results, and 19 studies indicate mixed results." — confidence(high)
  - Park & Irwin's qualification — "Despite the positive evidence on the profitability of technical trading strategies, most empirical studies are subject to various problems in their testing procedures, e.g. data snooping, ex post selection of trading rules or search technologies, and difficulties in estimation of risk and transaction costs." — confidence(high)
- **base_rate_stated**: **YES, in the sense that matters.** STW's Reality Check *is* a base rate for the search: it asks what the best-of-7,846 would look like under the null. The gap between the nominal p-value (the winner alone) and the Reality Check p-value (the winner priced against the search) is exactly the base-rate correction this file is about, and STW's own sentence is the cleanest statement of the principle anywhere in the literature: *"Such increases in the p-value, in the absence of improvements over the best performing trading rule, vividly illustrate the importance of jointly considering all the trading rules when drawing conclusions about the performance of the best-performing trading rule."*
- **detection_notes**: A pattern detector with tunable tolerances IS a rule universe. Every tolerance knob (neckline slope tolerance, symmetry tolerance, minimum base weeks, depth band, volume multiple) multiplies the search space. If a detector's parameters were chosen by looking at outcomes, its published hit rate carries the data-snooping bias STW quantified, and the honest correction is either a Reality Check / multiple-testing adjustment or a held-out period the parameters never saw. **A detector must record how many configurations were evaluated before the shipped one was chosen.** Without that count, no significance claim about it is computable.
- **conflicts**: STW find BLL's *in-sample* result survives snooping correction (*"their result... stands up to inspection for data-snooping effects"*) while the *out-of-sample* result reverses completely. Both are STW's own findings on adjacent data. Record both — the pattern "robust in-sample, dead out-of-sample" is the single most reproducible finding in this literature.

## 13. Base-rate contamination — a win rate without its unconditional benchmark

- **source(s)**: Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000) — the canonical study that reports **both**; Savin, Weller & Zvingelis (2006) — reports a conditional return **and** the unconditional equity premium in the same sentence; PatternFetch (2026) — a self-published commercial study that reports both, per category.
- **definition**: The methodological point, in LMW's own words: *"If technical patterns are informative, conditioning on them should alter the empirical distribution of returns; if the information contained in such patterns has already been incorporated into returns, the conditional and unconditional distribution of returns should be close."* And operationally: *"If conditioning on these technical patterns provides no incremental information, the quantiles of the conditional returns should be similar to those of unconditional returns."* A pattern's hit rate is therefore meaningless in isolation — the quantity of interest is hit rate **minus** the unconditional rate over the same universe, same horizon, same period.
- **published_rates**:
  - LMW sample — a large number of U.S. stocks, NYSE/AMEX and Nasdaq, 1962 to 1996, CRSP daily; 50 stocks per five-year subperiod × 7 subperiods per exchange — "apply this method to a large number of U.S. stocks from 1962 to 1996 to evaluate the effectiveness to technical analysis" — confidence(high)
  - LMW verdict — "By comparing the unconditional empirical distribution of daily stock returns to the conditional distribution—conditioned on specific technical indicators such as head-and-shoulders or double-bottoms—we find that over the 31-year sample period, several technical indicators do provide incremental information and may have some practical value." — confidence(high)
  - LMW's own caveat that informativeness ≠ profit — "Although this is a weaker test of the effectiveness of technical analysis—informativeness does not guarantee a profitable trading strategy—it is, nevertheless, a natural first step" and, in the conclusion, "While this does not necessarily imply that technical analysis can be used to generate 'excess' trading profits, it does raise the possibility that technical analysis can add value to the investment process." — confidence(high)
  - Savin/Weller/Zvingelis, conditional result — risk-adjusted excess returns of 5-7% per year to an H&S-conditioned strategy — S&P 500 and Russell 2000, 1990-1999 — "With data from the S&P 500 and the Russell 2000 over the period 1990-1999 we find strong evidence that the pattern had power to predict excess returns. Risk-adjusted excess returns to a trading strategy conditioned on 'head-and-shoulders' price patterns are 5-7 percent per year." — confidence(high)
  - **Savin et al.'s explicit unconditional benchmark, in the same paragraph as the result** — 11.4% per annum equity premium — same sample period — "This provides a strong indication that the HS strategy is successfully timing short sales, since a policy of purely random short sales with the probability of selecting a stock weighted by market capitalization would earn the negative of the equity premium, which in our sample period is 11.4 per cent per annum." — confidence(high)
  - Savin et al., the raw (unadjusted) number that the risk adjustment rescues — raw excess return −3.5% p.a. vs risk-adjusted +4.8% p.a. at the 60-day horizon — "the 60-day raw excess return for bandwidth multiple 2.5 is 0.0083 (see Table I) which translates into a raw excess return of – 3.5 per cent per annum (250/60 x 0.83 per cent) and a risk-adjusted excess return of 4.8 per cent as reported above." — confidence(high)
  - PatternFetch baseline construction — "For each market, timeframe and horizon, the harness measures the unconditional outcome: over all non-overlapping 10-bar forward windows in the same corpus, what fraction closed up, down, or exactly flat." — confidence(med — self-published commercial source, method stated but not independently verified)
  - PatternFetch measured drift baseline, US equities — 52.7% (hourly) to 61.2% (weekly) of pattern-free 10-bar windows closed higher — 117 US stocks and ETFs, snapshot 2026-07-18 — "52.7% on the hourly to 61.2% on the weekly" — confidence(med)
  - PatternFetch, head & shoulders daily, high-confidence band — 42.0% conditional vs 42.0% baseline, +0.0pp, ±3.1pp — n = 2,795 — "head & shoulders | 2,795 | 42.0% | 42.0% | +0.0pp | ±3.1pp | No lift" — confidence(med)
  - PatternFetch, double bottom daily, high-confidence band — 56.9% conditional vs 57.8% baseline, −0.9pp, ±2.8pp — n = 6,692 — "double bottom | 6,692 | 56.9% | 57.8% | −0.9pp | ±2.8pp | No lift" — confidence(med)
  - PatternFetch, overall — 0 of 105 categories survive correction — 373,748 non-overlapping occurrences, 117 US stocks/ETFs + 10 crypto pairs, 4 timeframes — "Across all 105 pattern × timeframe × confidence-band categories — 373,748 non-overlapping occurrences across 117 US stocks and ETFs and 10 crypto pairs — not one category's lift over its own pattern-free baseline survives correction" and "0 survive a Bonferroni correction for 105 tests — chance alone predicts ≈5.3" — confidence(med)
  - PatternFetch on the incoherence of published rates — "The same bearish engulfing is advertised at 57%, 75.76% and 79% by three widely-cited sources" — confidence(med)
- **base_rate_stated**: **YES — all three.** These are the only sources in this file that put the pattern number and the unconditional number in the same table. LMW's entire test statistic is a distance between conditional and unconditional distributions. Savin et al. name the 11.4% equity premium as the thing random short sales would have earned. PatternFetch computes a per-market, per-timeframe, per-horizon pattern-free baseline and reports lift, not hit rate. **Every other source in this file publishes a rate with no benchmark.**
- **detection_notes**: This is the engineering requirement that falls out of the whole file. A detector must compute, for its own universe and window, the unconditional probability of the same outcome over the same forward horizon, and publish `lift = conditional − unconditional` with a confidence interval, not `hit rate`. The baseline must be built from **non-overlapping** forward windows in the same corpus — overlapping windows inflate the effective n and shrink the interval fraudulently. Note the direction of the trap: because US equities drift up, a long-side pattern with a 57% hit rate on a 61% baseline is a **negative-lift** signal that will read as a 57% winner on any UI that omits the baseline.
- **conflicts**: **Savin/Weller/Zvingelis directly contradict Bulkowski on head-and-shoulders**, and say so by name: *"we find no evidence that a trading strategy based on HS patterns alone would be profitable, and for the second group, which consists of smaller stocks, the evidence is weak. Thus our results provide no support for the more extreme claims of some technical analysts, including Bulkowski, that trades based on the occurrence of HS patterns alone are consistently profitable."* Bulkowski's hst.html publishes a 19% break-even failure rate and 16% average decline on "more than 2,800 perfect trades". **Record both.** They are not the same measurement — Bulkowski measures move-to-ultimate-low on hand-vetted patterns, Savin et al. measure risk-adjusted excess return on an algorithmic population — and that is precisely why neither number licenses the other's claim. PatternFetch's H&S daily result (+0.0pp lift, n=2,795) is a third, again-different measurement that agrees with Savin et al.'s direction.

## 14. Replication failure of the pattern edge

- **source(s)**: Nekrasov, V. (2010), *Reproducing the "Foundations of Technical Analysis"* — **self-published, not peer-reviewed; I flag this because it is the only direct replication attempt of LMW I could find and its status matters.**
- **definition**: Failure here is the disappearance of a previously published statistical result when the same method is applied to a later period and different universes.
- **published_rates**:
  - Verdict — "I try to reproduce the results of Lo et al for the timespan from 1995 to 2010 (historical data for DJ30, SP100, NASDAQ100) and for the timespan from 2003 to 2010 (historical data for 10 stocks from DAX). I come to the conclusion that the results are not anymore reproducible." — confidence(med — the finding is clearly stated; the source is unrefereed)
  - Significance count, optimal bandwidth — 1 of 10 patterns (RTOP) — DJ30, Jan 1995-Dec 2010 — "in the most cases the p-values, which I obtain, are not small enough to reject the null hypothesis that the unconditional returns and the returns conditioned on patterns come from the same distribution. For the optimal bandwidth the only exception is RTOP" — confidence(med)
  - Significance count, LMW's 0.3× bandwidth — 2 of 10 (BTOP and RTOP) — same — "for 0.3*optimal bandwidth are BTOP and RTOP" — confidence(med)
  - Head-and-shoulders, DJ30 1995-2010 — n = 145 occurrences, K-S p = 0.1388 on normalised returns (0.4522 on non-normalised) — from the author's Table 1 — confidence(med — read from a table, transcribed not quoted)
  - Combined DJ30 + NASDAQ100 + SP500, H&S — K-S p = 0.1255 — author's Table 6 — confidence(med — table)
  - Comparison against LMW's own result — LMW found 5 of 10 significant on NYSE/AMEX by K-S; this replication found 1 of 10 — "Oppositely, Lo et all yield significant KS test statistic for five patterns: HS, BBOT, RTOP, RBOT and DTOP." — confidence(med)
- **base_rate_stated**: **YES** — the replication inherits LMW's design, so the unconditional return distribution is the explicit null throughout. Every p-value quoted is a test against it.
- **detection_notes**: The mechanism of the disappearance matters more than the fact of it. The author reports that his detector found **far more** patterns per stock-year than LMW's — e.g. 9.06 H&S per stock-year on DJ30 versus LMW's 0.61 in the largest quintile — a ~15× difference in detection density from the same published algorithm. **Two implementations of one published detector differed by an order of magnitude in how often they fired.** For an engineer this is the headline: pattern counts are not portable across implementations, so neither are hit rates computed from them. Any published rate must be paired with a detection density (patterns per symbol per year) or it cannot be compared to anything.
- **conflicts**: The replication contradicts LMW on nine of ten patterns. Both stand: LMW measured 1962-1996 CRSP with a hand-tuned bandwidth; Nekrasov measured 1995-2010 index constituents with an automatically-selected bandwidth and a different extrema routine. Note the plausible reading — that the edge was arbitraged away after 1996 — is exactly what Park & Irwin's survey anticipates when it says modern studies find profits *"at least until the early 1990s"*.

## 15. Detection-side failure — repainting and look-ahead bias

- **source(s)**: TradingView, Pine Script Docs, *Repainting* (primary for the platform, and the most widely-read statement of the problem); PatternFetch (2026) for a measured estimate of the bias size.
- **definition**: Verbatim, repainting is *"Script behavior causing historical vs realtime calculations or plots to behave differently."* The docs classify it in three tiers: *widespread but often acceptable* (using unconfirmed-bar values), *potentially misleading* (plotting into the past; calculating differently on realtime vs historical bars), and *unacceptable* (*"leaking future information into past bars"*). The mechanism, verbatim: *"On realtime bars, the high, low and close values are not fixed; they can change values many times before the realtime bar closes and its HLC values are fixed."* And the classic look-ahead: *"When request.security() is used with lookahead = barmerge.lookahead_on to fetch prices without offsetting the series by [1], it will return data from the future on historical bars, which is dangerously misleading."*
- **published_rates**:
  - Inflation of measured hit rate from starting the forward window at the pivot rather than at the bar the pattern becomes knowable — roughly 10 percentage points — measured on the PatternFetch corpus (373,748 occurrences, 117 US stocks/ETFs + 10 crypto pairs, 4 timeframes, snapshot 2026-07-18) — "Starting the forward window at the pivot itself — the convention in a great many published backtests — leaks those confirming bars into the result and inflates the measured hit rate by roughly 10 percentage points." — confidence(med — self-published commercial source; the magnitude is plausible and the mechanism is unambiguous, but it is one study)
  - Proportion of published indicators that repaint — **value: null** — `missing:` a search summary attributed a "more than 95% of indicators" figure to TradingView; **I did not find that sentence in the fetched Pine Script docs and do not record it as a finding.**
- **base_rate_stated**: Not applicable in the pattern sense — but note that PatternFetch's ~10pp figure IS itself a base-rate correction: it is the difference between two measurements of the same population differing only in when the clock starts.
- **detection_notes**: The mechanism is specific and every multi-bar pattern detector is exposed to it. A base or a head-and-shoulders is defined by **pivot highs and lows**, and a pivot is only confirmable *k* bars after it occurs. If a detector timestamps the pattern at the pivot bar, it has back-dated a detection by *k* bars and every outcome measured from that timestamp includes *k* bars of already-known price. The rules that follow: (a) timestamp every detection at the **bar it became knowable**, never at the pivot; (b) never let the current, unclosed bar contribute to pattern geometry; (c) never revise a past detection's geometry when later bars arrive — if the shape changes, that is a *new* detection, and the old one must remain in the record with its original timestamp; (d) any smoothing (kernel, MA, ZigZag) that is centred or two-sided is a look-ahead filter and must be replaced by a causal one or lagged by its half-width. Point (c) is the one most often violated in production: a ZigZag-based detector that redraws its last leg is repainting, and every backtest run over its output is fiction.
- **conflicts**: None — this is a mechanism, not a contested measurement.

## 16. Detection-side failure — over-fitted tolerances and smoothing choices

- **source(s)**: Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000), footnote 5; Savin, Weller & Zvingelis (2006), §C; Nekrasov (2010).
- **definition**: The tolerances that turn a shape into a detector (symmetry percentages, smoothing bandwidth, window length, extrema definition) are free parameters. When they are chosen by looking at the data, the detector is fitted, and its statistics carry the bias STW quantified in mode 12.
- **published_rates**:
  - LMW's bandwidth was chosen **by eye** — verbatim: *"Specifically, we produced fitted curves for various bandwidths and compared their extrema to the original price series visually to see if we were fitting more 'noise' than 'signal,' and asked several professional technical analysts to do the same. Through this informal process, we settled on the bandwidth of 0.3 x h and used it for the remainder of our analysis. This procedure was followed before we performed the statistical analysis of Section III, and we made no revision to the choice of bandwidth afterwards."* — confidence(high). To their considerable credit the last sentence is a pre-registration; note how rare that is.
  - LMW's H&S tolerance — the two shoulders within 1.5% of their average and the two troughs within 1.5% of their average — "E1 and E5 within 1.5 percent of their average", "E2 and E4 within 1.5 percent of their average" (OCR-garbled in the scan but legible); rectangles use 0.75% — confidence(med — the numbers are legible, the surrounding notation is OCR-damaged)
  - LMW window and lag — rolling windows of 38 trading days, pattern completion detected with a 3-day lag — "Lo et al. (2000) analyze the data using rolling windows of span n = 38 trading days" (Savin et al. describing LMW) — confidence(high)
  - Savin et al.: effect of tightening tolerances toward Bulkowski's visual criteria — population fell 30%, predictive power unchanged — Russell 2000, 1990-1999, 60 trading days, bandwidth multiple 2.5 — "the number of HS patterns detected drops from 6,406 to 4,452. This is a decrease of 30 per cent." and "we find no evidence to suggest that fine tuning the algorithm with the calibrations based on Bulkowski adds anything to its predictive power." — confidence(high)
  - Nekrasov: bandwidth sensitivity — "Trying to multiply the optimal bandwidth with 0.3, as Lo et al do, does not help out. It leads to undersmoothing" and "with the bandwidth equal to 0.3h it tends to detecting of too local extrema" — confidence(med)
  - Nekrasov: implementation sensitivity within one language — "when I engage ksmooth {stats} (another R routine for kernel smoothing) with the same bandwidth and kernel as in npreg I get significantly different results. This lies, most likely, on fine differences in program implementation." — confidence(med)
- **base_rate_stated**: Not applicable to the tolerance question directly; but the Savin et al. finding is the crucial one and it IS a controlled comparison — same data, same period, two tolerance settings, outcome held up against the same factor benchmark.
- **detection_notes**: The single most useful engineering result in this file is Savin et al.'s: **tightening a detector's tolerances toward an expert's own hand-drawn criteria removed 30% of the detections and improved nothing.** A "high confidence" tier built from tighter tolerances is therefore not entitled to claim a better hit rate until that claim is measured, and PatternFetch's high-confidence bands showing zero and negative lift (mode 13) is consistent with it. Second: Nekrasov's finding that two smoothing routines in the same language, with the same kernel and bandwidth, gave "significantly different results" means a detector's output is a property of its implementation, not of the pattern definition — so cross-vendor comparisons of pattern statistics are invalid unless both detectors were run on the same bars.
- **conflicts**: None on the measurement; a philosophical conflict between the practitioner tradition (tighter criteria = better patterns, asserted) and the one study that tested it (tighter criteria = fewer patterns, same predictive power, measured). Record both, and note which one has a sample.

## 17. Detection-side failure — visually obvious, statistically indistinguishable from noise

- **source(s)**: Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000), Tables I and II with the simulated-GBM control row; Bulkowski's "perfect patterns" framing (uglypatterns.html, per-pattern pages); Bulkowski, Pattern Frequency Study.
- **definition**: The question is whether a pattern occurs more often in real prices than in a random walk calibrated to the same mean and variance. If the counts match, the shape is a property of noise and any statistics conditioned on it describe the noise.
- **published_rates**:
  - LMW's simulated control — for each stock in each five-year subperiod, geometric Brownian motion "calibrated to match the mean and standard deviation of each stock in each five-year subperiod" — confidence(high)
  - H&S and inverted H&S: real vs simulated counts — 1,611 and 1,654 real vs 577 and 578 simulated — NYSE/AMEX, 1962-1996 — "the simulated sample has only 577 head-and-shoulders and 578 inverted-head-and-shoulders patterns, whereas the actual data have considerably more, 1,611 and 1,654, respectively." — confidence(high)
  - Broadening tops and bottoms: real vs simulated — 725 and 748 real vs 1,227 and 1,028 simulated — **more patterns in the random walk than in the data** — same sample — "for broadening tops and bottoms, the simulated sample contains many more occurrences than the actual data, 1,227 and 1,028 as compared to 725 and 748, respectively." — confidence(high)
  - Triangles — "roughly comparable across the two samples" — same — confidence(high)
  - LMW's own caution about the control — "Of course, the simulated sample is only one realization of geometric Brownian motion, so it is difficult to draw general conclusions about the relative frequencies." — confidence(high)
  - Bulkowski's population is hand-vetted — the site's statistics are repeatedly qualified as "perfect trades" (2,800+ for H&S tops; 3,000+ for symmetrical triangles; 1,400+ for ascending triangles) and he maintains a separate category for patterns that *"differs significantly from the normal appearance of the chart pattern"* — "An ugly chart pattern is one that differs significantly from the normal appearance of the chart pattern." — confidence(high)
  - Comparative performance of ugly vs perfect patterns — **value: null** — `missing:` the ugly-patterns page states *"I only have one pattern completed so far"*. **The selection effect implied by 'perfect' is acknowledged on the site and has not been measured.**
  - Bulkowski's frequency study universe — 500 stocks, July 1991 to July 1996, bull market only — "I scanned my database of 500 stocks during the bull market of July 1991 to July 1996." — confidence(high)
- **base_rate_stated**: **YES for LMW** — the simulated-GBM row *is* the base rate for pattern *occurrence*, and it is published in the same table as the real counts. **NO for Bulkowski** — no random-walk control appears anywhere on the site, and the sample is explicitly a hand-selected "perfect" subset drawn substantially from a single bull market.
- **detection_notes**: Two concrete requirements. First, **every detector should be run against a phase-randomised or GBM-simulated version of its own universe, and the pattern counts compared.** If your cup-with-handle fires as often on simulated data as on real data, the detector is a noise oscillator with a name, and no downstream statistic can rescue it — LMW's broadening-top result (more in the random walk than in the data) is the worked example. Second, the "perfect pattern" problem: a detector that ships strict tolerances and reports statistics from the resulting population is reporting a survivor-selected number, and it must publish the selection rate (what fraction of candidate shapes it accepted) so a reader can see how much of the universe was discarded to produce the number.
- **conflicts**: LMW's GBM control finds H&S ~2.8× more frequent in real data than in the random walk — evidence the shape is *not* pure noise — while Nekrasov's replication (mode 14) found the returns after those shapes indistinguishable from unconditional returns. **These are not in conflict: a pattern can be non-random in occurrence and uninformative about subsequent returns.** That distinction is the one most often collapsed in marketing copy.

## Implications for an honest detector

**What the detector must NOT claim.**

1. **Never attach a published failure rate to a live detection.** Every published rate in this file was measured on a different universe, a different period, a different entry rule, and — in Bulkowski's case — a hand-vetted "perfect" subset drawn largely from bull markets. Bulkowski's own data show the same pattern's failure rate moving from 37.4% to 17.9% purely by changing the entry rule, and his 10%-failure series moving from 11% to 44% purely by changing the year.
2. **Never publish a hit rate without the unconditional base rate.** This is the one non-negotiable. US equities drift up; PatternFetch measured pattern-free 10-bar windows closing higher 52.7% (hourly) to 61.2% (weekly) of the time. A long-side pattern advertised at 57% is, on those numbers, a losing bet against doing nothing. Publish `lift = conditional − unconditional` with an interval, computed on **non-overlapping** windows in the **same corpus**, or publish nothing.
3. **Never present a "high confidence" tier as a better tier without measuring it.** Savin et al. tightened an H&S detector toward Bulkowski's own criteria, lost 30% of detections (6,406 → 4,452) and gained nothing in predictive power. Tighter tolerances are a smaller sample, not a better signal, until proven otherwise.
4. **Never present "confirmed on heavy volume" as a quality upgrade.** The one study that measured it on 8,000 patterns found failures at 14% on above-average breakout volume versus 5% below, for a mean-move gain of 1.8 percentage points.
5. **Never average two sources' rates for the same named pattern.** Bulkowski's own two pages disagree on triple-top bust frequency (19% vs ~29%) and neither states a sample size. Bulkowski and Savin et al. disagree on whether head-and-shoulders is tradeable at all. Store both, cite both, average neither.
6. **Never claim a base-stage number implies elevated risk.** O'Neil's third/fourth-stage sell rule and Minervini's "Base 4 and 5 fail more frequently" are assertions; no reachable source attaches a sample to either.
7. **Never say a pattern "works" on the strength of a non-random occurrence count.** LMW showed H&S occurs ~2.8× more often than in a matched random walk *and* that its return significance flips between exchanges and between two tests on the same data. Occurrence is not information.

**States the detector must distinguish.**

| State | Entry condition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `forming` | geometry complete, no confirming close beyond the boundary | Must never be counted in any published hit rate. Bulkowski: 48% of unconfirmed double bottoms go on to close below the second bottom. |
| `triggered` | first **close** beyond the pattern boundary, timestamped at the bar it became knowable, not at the pivot | Using the intraday high instead of the close silently makes the test easier. Timestamping at the pivot inflates the measured hit rate by roughly 10pp (PatternFetch). |
| `threw_back` / `pulled_back` | returned to the breakout price within 30 days (Bulkowski's window) | An **attribute**, not a failure. Do not flip state on it. |
| `busted` | moved ≤10% in the breakout direction, then closed on the opposite side of the pattern | Causally computable with no look-ahead. This is the cleanest terminal-failure signal in the literature. Track single/double/triple busts separately — the sources report them separately and their subsequent moves differ. |
| `failed` | the detector's own stated stop or invalidation level was closed through | Must be defined by the product, stated in the UI, and used consistently in every published statistic. Do not import Bulkowski's 5% break-even definition here — it has no time bound and cannot be evaluated live. |
| `expired` | horizon elapsed without resolution | See below. |

Add two orthogonal fields to every record, because the evidence says outcomes are conditional on them: **market regime** at trigger time (index vs long MA, distance from high, FTD/distribution state) and **universe** (exchange, cap band) — LMW's answer flipped entirely between NYSE/AMEX and Nasdaq on identical detectors.

**What the detector must refuse to publish without a measured base rate.**

Any hit rate, win rate, success rate, failure rate, "accuracy", target-attainment percentage, or confidence score. Each of these is a conditional probability whose complement is meaningless alone. If the baseline has not been computed on the same corpus and horizon, the field should render as "not measured" — not as a number with a caveat, because users read the number and skip the caveat. Three additional things must be recorded or the numbers are not interpretable at all: (a) **detection density** — patterns per symbol per year, because Nekrasov's implementation of LMW's published algorithm fired ~15× more often than LMW's own (9.06 vs 0.61 H&S per stock-year), so counts are not portable between implementations; (b) **selection rate** — what fraction of candidate shapes the tolerances accepted, because Bulkowski's numbers are drawn from "perfect" patterns and he has not measured the ugly ones; (c) **configurations evaluated** — how many tolerance settings were tried before shipping, because that count is the multiple-testing denominator STW showed drives the whole significance calculation (their p-value for the best rule alone was 0.05; priced against the search, ~0.15).

**How long a detection should stay active before expiring.**

The evidence gives three usable anchors and no single answer, so state the choice explicitly rather than inheriting one.

- **30 calendar days** is the only explicit time bound in Bulkowski's vocabulary — it is the throwback/pullback window. It is a defensible expiry for "is this breakout still the breakout".
- **38 to 60 trading days** is the horizon in the academic H&S work: LMW use rolling windows of 38 trading days with a 3-day completion lag; Savin et al. found their significant intercepts at the **60-day** horizon and measured over one, two and three months. If you want a horizon with published support, 60 trading days is the best-attested.
- **10 bars** is PatternFetch's forward window, and the level at which they found zero lift across 105 categories.
- The **unbounded** convention — Bulkowski's breakout-to-ultimate-high — is not implementable live and must not be used for anything a user sees. It is why his average rises are large and why they cannot be compared to any fixed-horizon number in this file.

Practical recommendation: expire a `triggered` detection at a stated fixed horizon (60 trading days has the most support), terminate it early on `busted` or `failed`, and never let a `forming` detection persist indefinitely — the shape that has not confirmed is, in Bulkowski's own framing, not a pattern yet, and 48% of the ones that never confirm go on to break their own low.

**The one-line version.** The literature contains a great many pattern rates and almost no base rates. Three sources put both in the same table — Lo/Mamaysky/Wang, Savin/Weller/Zvingelis, and PatternFetch — and in all three the honest number is much smaller than the advertised one, or zero. A detector that publishes the advertised number is not reporting a measurement; it is repeating a claim.
