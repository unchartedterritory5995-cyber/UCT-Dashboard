## Pocket Pivot

- **origin / source_name**: Dr. Chris Kacher (solely), 2005. Published in Gil Morales & Chris Kacher, *Trade Like an O'Neil Disciple: How We Made Over 18,000% in the Stock Market* (Wiley, 2010) — the pocket pivot / breakout-buy-point chapter, and p. 160 (cited by the authors themselves). Maintained on virtueofselfishinvesting.com, which the authors call "the original home of the pocket pivot buy point."

- **definition**: An early, in-base entry that detects institutional accumulation *inside* a consolidation rather than at the new-high breakout. In the authors' words: *"Institutional Buying creates new-high base breakouts, but we also know that institutional buying occurs within consolidations and during uptrends."* … *"This buying within consolidations and uptrends in most cases leaves price/volume 'footprints'."* … *"The pocket pivot describes that 'footprint,' and provides a clear, buyable 'pivot point,' or 'pocket pivot buy point.'"* It was created because *"the sideways choppy markets of 2004-2005 were making base breakouts fail."*

- **criteria**:
  - up-day volume vs prior down-day volume — **volume(t) > MAX(volume of all DOWN-close days in the 10 trading days immediately prior to t)** — *"The day's volume should be larger than the highest down volume day over the prior 10 days."* — high
  - the comparison is to the MAXIMUM, not the mean/median, of down-day volume in the window — max — *"If volume today exceeds the largest down volume day over the past 10 days, it would be a pocket pivot."* — high
  - lookback window length — **10 trading days, prior to (i.e. excluding) the signal day** — *"over the prior 10 days"* / *"over the past 10 days"* — high. **Note:** every primary VoSI statement says "prior 10 days" or "past 10 days"; none of the fetched primary text says whether the signal day itself is inside the window. Reading it as *prior* (exclusive) is the only reading consistent with "up day vs prior down days," but it is an inference. `missing: an explicit statement of whether day t is included in the 10-day window.`
  - base context — must be inside or emerging from a constructive base — *"As with base breakouts, proper pocket pivots should emerge within or out of constructive basing patterns."* — high
  - fundamentals — no numeric threshold published — value: null — *"The stock's fundamentals should be strong, i.e., excellent earnings, sales, pretax margins, ROE, strong leader in its space, etc."* — high (for the requirement) / `missing: numeric thresholds for earnings growth, sales growth, pretax margin, ROE — the authors publish only the adjective "excellent".`
  - moving-average context after a breakout — the 10-day simple moving average (10-dma) — *"If the pocket pivot occurs in an uptrend after the stock has broken out, it should act constructively around its 10-dma."* — high
  - extension test — a pivot beginning **above** the 10-dma is normally extended — value: null (no percentage published) — *"Page 160 of our book says that pocket pivots that start above their 10dma can in most cases be considered extended."* and *"Some pocket pivots may occur after the stock is extended from the base. If the pivot occurs right near its 10-dma, it can be bought, otherwise it is extended and should be avoided."* — high. `missing: a published tolerance (in % or ATR) for how far above the 10-dma still counts as "right near" it.`
  - a worked extension example the authors gave — the whole day's LOW above the 10-dma ⇒ mildly extended — *"We did not notify members on 2/8/12 since KORS could be considered mildly extended since the low of the trading day was above its 10dma."* — high. This is the closest thing to a computable extension test in the fetched corpus.
  - close location in the day's range — top of range preferred; **top half** is the stated minimum tolerance — *"We ideally prefer the stock to close the day at the top end of its trading range. However, if it closes in the top half of its trading range and is up on the day, it could still be considered valid."* — high
  - closing above the 10-dma is NOT strictly required — value: null — *"Some pocket pivots are of high enough quality to be considered even if the price does not close above the 10dma. It is contextual with the overall chart."* — high
  - substitute moving average when the stock disrespects the 10-dma — the 20-day **exponential** moving average (20-dema) — *"You could switch to using the 20-dema instead since it is less likely to violate it."* — high
  - where the buy point is — the pocket pivot day's **closing price** — *"With pocket pivots, the pivot point is often the closing price of the day on which the pocket pivot occurs."* — high
  - entry may be next day — *"You will receive an email at the end of the trading day after we have done our analysis, and can thus buy these stocks the next trading day."* — high
  - later-era modification of the entry (post-2013) — buy weakness, not strength — value: null (threshold not published) — *"today's markets (2013-2017) are far more unforgiving thus we have counseled members to buy on constructive weakness as opposed to strength"* — high
  - stop-loss (max risk) — **7-8%** — *"Most investors use a 7-8% maximum stop loss. Always adhere to that first."* — high
  - stop-loss (typical, under the revised buy-weakness entry) — **less than 3%** — *"This minimises any losses such that we are able to keep sell stops to typically less than 3%."* — high

- **measured_performance**:
  - **"In practice, in an uptrending market, about half of high quality stocks showing pocket pivots will work."** — This is an **assertion**, not a measurement: no sample size, no date range, no universe definition, no trade-management assumption is published alongside it.
  - The **only benchmark given** is itself unquantified: *"In the 1990s, this was roughly equivalent to the success rate of standard base breakouts during uptrending markets, except with pocket pivots, the losses are often contained to within 5% or less compared to buying breakouts."* So the "~50%" figure has **no independently published base rate** — the comparison partner (base-breakout success) carries no number either. **Flagged explicitly per the corpus rule.**
  - Elsewhere the authors give a *different* comparative claim with no number: *"the success rate tends to be better than that of base breakouts since buying on a breakout is often too late."* (See **conflicts** — this contradicts "roughly equivalent.")
  - Backtest description, **no results published**: *"I backtested the pocket pivot trading strategy going back to the 1920s."* … *"The method not only worked in the sideways choppy markets of 2004-2006 but also in the 1990s and in entirely different eras such as the roaring 20s."* No sample size, no win rate, no average gain, no drawdown, no methodology detail beyond chart inspection: *"having examined millions of charts over the last 20+ years."* Treat as **anecdotal / not a measured result.**
  - The authors **explicitly refuse** to publish aggregate performance: *"It is simple-minded to want to know the 'performance' of pocket pivot buy points and reflects a lack of understanding of what a pocket pivot is and how it should be used."* … *"pocket pivots are not an investment strategy, they are a technical tool, hence the idea that they embody some sort of aggregate 'performance' is silly at best."*
  - Single-name anecdote, n=1: MCP (2010) *"went on to gain as much as +91.1% based on the closing price of its pocket pivot."* No denominator; the same page notes MCP's *first* pocket pivot *"was premature to buy."*

- **invalidation** — the authors' explicit "do not buy / ignore it" list:
  - *"Do not buy pocket pivots if the overall chart formation is in a multi-month downtrend (5 months or longer)."* — the **5-month** figure is exact and published.
  - *"Do not buy pocket pivots if the stock is under a critical moving average such as the 50-dma or 200-dma."*
  - *"Do not buy pocket pivots if the stock formed a 'V' where it sells off hard down through the 10-dma or 50-dma then shoots straight back up."* Elaborated: *"a stock that goes straight down well below its 10dma, then straight back up its their 10dma might be avoided since this type of action can be failure prone."*
  - *"Avoid buying pocket pivots that occur after wedging patterns."* Wedging is defined in a secondary VoSI-derived restatement as *"stock inches up day by day without significant volume or with declining volume"* — the primary Ten Rules page states the prohibition without defining wedging. `missing: a computable definition of "wedging" from the authors' own text.`
  - Extended from the base and not near the 10-dma ⇒ *"it is extended and should be avoided."*
  - A single anomalous high-volume down day inside the window legitimately kills the signal, and the authors say **do not soften it**: *"It is best to use a purist approach and avoid the stock. In a good market, there will be more than a few buying opportunities."*
  - **But** — and this directly undercuts mechanical computation — they also permit discretionary exclusion of a down-volume day: *"Context is important so in some cases such as this one, you could disregard this as a red volume day."* (in a case where a high-volume down day was judged accumulation, not distribution).
  - Failure/sell trigger: a **"violation" of the 10-dma**, precisely defined — *"A violation occurs when the stock closes below the 10dma, then in subsequent days, trades below the low of that day."*

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV):
  - Primitives: `close[t] > close[t-1]` (up day); `down_day(i) = close[i] < close[i-1]`; `max(volume[i] for i in [t-10, t-1] if down_day(i))`; SMA(close,10), SMA(close,50), SMA(close,200), EMA(close,20); `(close[t]-low[t])/(high[t]-low[t]) >= 0.5` for the top-half-of-range test; `low[t] > SMA10[t]` for the "mildly extended" test.
  - Base-context detection is required and is the hard part: "constructive basing pattern," "multi-month downtrend (5 months or longer)," "V" recovery, and "wedging" all need a base/trend classifier. The 5-month downtrend test is the only one with a published number and is approximable (e.g. price below a declining longer MA, or lower highs, over ~105 trading days) — but the authors publish no formula, so any implementation is **your** definition, not theirs.
  - **Edge case not specified by the source**: what happens when the prior-10-day window contains **zero** down-close days. The rule's right-hand side is then undefined (max of an empty set). The authors never address it. Decide explicitly and record the decision as yours.
  - **Ambiguity**: "down volume day" is stated as a day the stock closed down. Whether the down/up classification is close-vs-prior-close or close-vs-open is never stated. Close-vs-prior-close is the conventional reading; flag as an implementation choice.
  - **NOT computable from daily OHLCV alone**: the fundamentals gate (earnings, sales, pretax margin, ROE, "leader in its space"); "constructive" as a qualitative judgement; the discretionary permission to disregard a red volume day; general-market context, which the authors treat as a first-class filter.

- **conflicts**:
  - **Against O'Neil / IBD on where you buy.** O'Neil-style buying is at the breakout through the top of a base; the pocket pivot is explicitly designed to fire *inside* the base, *before* the breakout — *"they get us into a stock early often before it breaks out of its base"* … *"a head start on buying a stock before the rest of the crowd comes in on the obvious breakout."* Kacher's stated reason for inventing it is that the O'Neil breakout was failing: *"the sideways choppy markets of 2004-2005 were making base breakouts fail."* The *volume primitive itself* differs in kind: O'Neil/IBD grade breakout volume against a **percentage above the 50-day average volume**, whereas the pocket pivot grades against the **maximum down-day volume of the prior 10 days** — a different denominator entirely. **I did not fetch an O'Neil/IBD primary source in this session, so I am recording no IBD number here; value: null on the O'Neil side.** `missing: an IBD/O'Neil primary citation for their breakout volume threshold, to be sourced in the O'Neil file of this corpus and cross-linked.`
  - **Internal contradiction inside Morales/Kacher's own material on the pocket pivot's edge vs breakouts.** Two published statements disagree:
    - *"In the 1990s, this was **roughly equivalent** to the success rate of standard base breakouts during uptrending markets"* (FAQ *What is the success rate of pocket pivots?*, first published 15 Sep 2010).
    - *"the success rate **tends to be better than** that of base breakouts since buying on a breakout is often too late"* (FAQ *Do you have performance results…*, first published 25 Feb 2014, last updated 20 Oct 2022).
    Record both; do not reconcile.
  - **Internal contradiction on whether a performance number exists at all.** One FAQ supplies "about half"; another calls asking for aggregate performance *"silly at best."* Both are the authors'.
  - **Against Minervini** on the trigger MA: Morales/Kacher anchor the pocket pivot to the **10-dma** (falling back to the **20-dema** when the 10-dma is disrespected, and to the **50-dma** as a wider sell guide). Minervini's low-cheat / VCP entries are not anchored to a 10-day line in the same way. `missing: a fetched Minervini primary source in this session — recorded as a structural difference only, with no Minervini number asserted.`

## Continuation Pocket Pivot (also "follow-on" pocket pivot)

- **origin / source_name**: Morales & Kacher, *Trade Like an O'Neil Disciple* — the chapter titled *Continuation Pocket Pivots: Using the 10-Day Moving Average* (chapter title confirmed from the O'Reilly TOC listing; **the chapter body itself was 403-blocked**). Elaborated across VoSI FAQs and PPR reports.

- **definition**: The same volume signature applied to a stock that has **already** broken out and is trending, used as an add point rather than an initial entry. *"The recent pocket pivot was a continuation pocket pivot thus occurs after the stock has broken out of its most recent base and is trending higher. It allows one to buy into a position even if they miss earlier pocket pivot buy points."*

- **criteria**:
  - volume rule — identical to the standard pocket pivot — *"If volume today exceeds the largest down volume day over the past 10 days, it would be a pocket pivot."* — high
  - location — after the most recent base breakout, in an uptrend — *"occurs after the stock has broken out of its most recent base and is trending higher"* — high
  - moving-average behaviour — must act constructively around the 10-dma — *"If the pocket pivot occurs in an uptrend after the stock has broken out, it should act constructively around its 10-dma."* — high
  - undercutting the 10-dma is tolerated if the volume signature holds — value: null — *"It can undercut its 10-dma as long as it shows resilience by showing volume that is greater than the highest down volume day over the prior 10 days."* — med (this phrasing appears in a search-engine rendering of VoSI FAQ text rather than in a page body I extracted verbatim; treat the *tolerance* as sourced, the exact wording as med)
  - efficacy claim vs initial entries — no number — value: null — *"This continuation buy point can work just as well as initial buy points, assuming the stock or ETF is a leader in its space."* — high
  - author's own ranking against neighbouring setups — no number — value: null — *"I would say I have slightly more comfort in buying continuation pocket pivots than 50d SMA bounces, 3 weeks tight, ants, and base breakouts, but all have their place."* — high
  - does **not** reset the 7-week clock — *"It would not be reset for a continuation (follow on) pocket pivot."* — high

- **measured_performance**: none published.

- **invalidation**: same disqualifiers as the standard pocket pivot. Additionally, a huge one-day move makes the add-point unusable on risk grounds: *"since SLV advanced 6.75% that day, if you bought at the top, your potential downside risk could be greater than your risk tolerance levels, in which case you should not be buying at those levels."* (6.75% is a worked example, not a published threshold.)

- **detection_notes**: identical volume primitive to the standard pocket pivot, plus a state flag "has broken out of the most recent base." The breakout-state flag is **not** derivable from OHLCV without a base detector; you need prior-base high, breakout date, and trend state. The distinction between "initial" and "continuation" pocket pivot is therefore entirely a function of your base-detection layer, not of the volume rule.

- **conflicts**: none found between the authors and others on this specific variant. Note the internal asymmetry: a continuation pocket pivot does **not** reset the 7-week clock, but a buyable gap-up **does**, and an in-base pocket pivot *"would usually"* — the three sibling signals are treated inconsistently by design.

## Buyable Gap-Up (BGU)

- **origin / source_name**: Morales & Kacher. Rules first set out in *Trade Like an O'Neil Disciple* (Wiley, 2010) — the authors themselves cite **page 171, hardcover 1st edition, "Rule #2"**. Revisited in *In the Trading Cockpit with the O'Neil Disciples* (Wiley, 2012/2013), Chapter 6, *Buyable Gap-Up Exercises*. Applied daily in VoSI's "BGU" report series.

- **definition**: A gap-up that is large enough and heavy enough on volume to be bought *into strength* on the gap day itself, with the gap day's own intraday low as the risk reference. The authors treat it as the same family as the pocket pivot: *"gap ups which are an extreme form of pocket pivot."* And: *"Base breakout and gap up buy points will be included as they technically fall under the pocket pivot concept."* Crucially, a true BGU does not fill: *"truly powerful buyable gap ups do not close their gap but continue higher from the gap up point."*

- **criteria**:
  - gap magnitude — **≥ 0.75 × the 40-day Average True Range, measured on the day before the gap-up day** — *"At least 0.75 times the stock's 40-day Average True Range of the day before the gap-up day."* — **med** (this is the *Trade Like an O'Neil Disciple* rule, reached through a secondary blog quoting the book; the O'Reilly and archive.org copies were blocked). **Corroborated by a primary VoSI real-time report using exactly this construct:** *"Ametek (AME), posted a buyable gap-up with a 2.27 point move on the open, much larger than its 40-day Average True Range of … on today's 2.27 of .89 (ATR based on yesterday's close)."* The parenthetical *"ATR based on yesterday's close"* independently confirms the "day before the gap-up day" anchoring — high confidence on the **40-day ATR anchored to the prior day**; med on the **0.75** multiplier specifically.
  - volume — **≥ 1.5× (150% of) the 50-day moving average of daily volume** — *"Volume is 1.5 times the 50-day SMA of daily trading volume."* / *"Occur on volume at least 1.5 times or 150% above the 50-day moving average of daily trading volume."* — **med** (secondary quoting the book; no primary VoSI page fetched in this session states the multiplier). **Note the two secondary phrasings are not the same claim**: "1.5 times" the average and "150% *above*" the average differ by a factor of ~1.67 (1.5× vs 2.5×). `missing: the book's exact sentence, to settle whether the threshold is 1.5× the 50-day average volume or 150% above it. Do not implement until resolved — the ambiguity is a factor of 1.67 on the gate.`
  - trend context — within an uptrend or constructive consolidation, not a downtrend — *"Occur within an uptrend or constructive consolidation, not during a downtrend."* — med (secondary)
  - prior advance — the stock should **normally have doubled** before the BGU — *"A stock should normally have doubled before a BGU to make the BGU more likely to succeed. If the stock is an IPO, the same applies. Some hot stocks double from their new issue price on the day they go public, so this would count as having doubled."* — **high** (primary FAQ). This is an exact, computable, and frequently-omitted criterion.
  - stop reference — **the intraday low of the gap-up day** — book Rule #2, quoted by the authors verbatim: *"In the days following the gap-up, sell if the stock trades below the intraday low of the gap-up day. One can allow the stock to close first before deciding whether to sell on an intraday move below the gap-up day's intraday low. Stocks with higher volatility can be given a little more room than those with lower volatility. Also watch for pullbacks just under the intraday low of the gap-up day that are close to and hence might find support at the 10-day, 50-day, or 200-day moving averages."* — **high**
  - porosity (allowed undercut of the gap-day low), general — **1-2%** — *"A 1-2% undercut can be permitted in some cases."* — high
  - porosity, alternative statement — **2-3%** — *"Intraday low of the gap-up day plus 2-3% more on the downside to allow the stock to dip below the intraday low."* — med (secondary) — and from a primary FAQ on a second gap-up: *"You would use the low of each gap up day as your sell stop, giving perhaps a 2-3% undercut of the low of the gap day as wiggle room, depending on the volatility of the stock."* — high. **Both 1-2% and 2-3% are published by the authors; they are not reconciled in the source.**
  - the **6-day sell rule** — after 5 days of trading, porosity collapses to ~zero from day 6 — *"The 6-day rule says that after a BGU, if the stock trades for 5 days then on the 6th day or later breaks below the low of the gap up day, the porosity which is the amount of room given for the stock to undercut the low of the gap up day should be minimal to zero. Normally, porosity for a stock that has gapped up is 1-2%, though contextual to the volatility of the stock."* — **high**. Exact, computable, and time-dependent.
  - maximum risk — **7-8%**, overriding the gap-low stop — *"If your maximum risk is 7-8%, then you adhere to that rule first, and perhaps not buy that gap up because it is beyond your risk level."* — high
  - permitted risk escalation in exceptional cases — **9-10%** — *"Alternatively, you could also create a rule that allows you to increase your maximum risk to 9-10% in such unusual cases if the stock gapping up is clearly a fundamentally and technically strong leader."* — high
  - entry timing — buy at least partly at the open; half/half is their suggestion — *"we have suggested one buy a half position at the open, and the other half later in the day, or in ensuing days if the stock has had a runaway gap."* — high
  - the gap stays buyable after day 1 — *"gap-ups remain buyable anywhere near or within that first-day's trading range"* even days later — med (rendered summary of the PPR psychology report; the underlying report's own line is *"one does not have to try and buy the lowest price within the gap-up day's range"* — high for that narrower claim)
  - **resets the 7-week clock** — *"It would be reset for a buyable gap up."* — high

- **measured_performance**: **none published.** No win rate, average gain, or failure rate for BGUs appears in any fetched source. The nearest thing is the unquantified comparative in the PPR psychology report — that gap-ups *appear* riskier than pocket pivots but *"this perception is not valid"* when analysing actual statistical outcomes — with **no statistics shown**. Flag: a claim about statistical outcomes with no statistics and no base rate.

- **invalidation** — when a BGU should be IGNORED or exited:
  - **The gap closes.** *"With FIO in today's trade, out of the starting gate, it sold off, then undercut the prior day's closing price. This is always a negative for any buyable gap up situation. The gap has been closed but truly powerful buyable gap ups do not close their gap but continue higher from the gap up point."* And: *"If the stock continues lower after closing the gap, it is generally best to sell since buyable gap ups do not close their gaps."*
  - **Insufficient volume on the day.** *"The risk in buying at the open is that the stock closes the gap or the stock does not trade sufficient volume to qualify as a BGU."* The BGU is therefore **not confirmed until the close** — an intraday gate.
  - **Extended after a long prior uptrend** — the authors' own worked refusal: *"While both qualify as buyable gap ups since both stocks have strong fundamentals, the LVS gap up strikes us as extended because it occurred after a nearly 4-month uptrend in the stock."* — the **~4-month uptrend** is the disqualifier they name, and note it fires **even when the mechanical BGU criteria are met.**
  - **Risk beyond tolerance** — if the gap day's low sits more than 7-8% below your fill, *"perhaps not buy that gap up because it is beyond your risk level."*
  - Trades below the gap-day intraday low in subsequent days (book Rule #2), subject to porosity and the 6-day rule.
  - Closing at the low of the day: *"If the stock does not close the gap but closes at the low of its trading range, one could either sell or use an undercutting of the low as their sell stop."*

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV):
  - Primitives: `gap = open[t] - close[t-1]` (the AME report measures the "move on the open," i.e. anchored to the prior close); `ATR(40)` evaluated at `t-1`; `SMA(volume, 50)`; `low[t]` retained as a persistent stop level; a day-counter since the gap for the 6-day rule; a "has doubled" test over the prior advance (needs a prior-low anchor — e.g. `high[t] / min(low) over the prior base/advance >= 2`, or IPO price for new issues).
  - The **doubled-before** criterion needs a low anchor the authors do not define. `missing: the lookback over which the doubling is measured (from the 52-week low? from the base low? from IPO?). For IPOs they say the new-issue price counts — daily OHLCV does not carry the IPO offer price.`
  - **NOT computable from daily OHLCV alone**:
    - Intraday confirmation. The authors buy *at the open* on a signal that is not confirmed until volume arrives; they explicitly reason about *"how much volume is coming into the stock during the first 5 min, 15 min, 30 min."* A daily-bar screener can only detect a BGU **after the close** — one full day later than they trade it.
    - The half-at-open / half-later entry, and the "runaway gap" judgement.
    - Whether the gap "closed" intraday (needs the intraday path, not just OHLC — though `low[t] <= close[t-1]` is a serviceable daily proxy and is exactly the FIO test the authors describe).
    - The fundamentals gate and the "leader in its space" judgement.
  - The **1.5× vs 150%-above** ambiguity above must be resolved before this is implementable at all.

- **conflicts**:
  - **Morales/Kacher vs Morales/Kacher on the ATR rule — and the direction is the opposite of what is usually assumed.** The ATR calculation is the **earlier** rule (2010, *Trade Like an O'Neil Disciple*), and the **later** book **relaxes it**: *"while previously a calculation required the gap-up to be at least 0.75 times the 40-day average true range of the stock in question, in practice it is enough to eyeball a gap-up move that appears to be of sufficient magnitude on a standard arithmetic daily chart."* (*In the Trading Cockpit*, via the secondary book review — flagged as secondary.) So the ATR-based version is not a later refinement layered on top; it is the original quantitative rule that the authors subsequently downgraded to a visual judgement, while **keeping** the volume rule as the binding one: *"Of more importance is the magnitude of volume present."* Record both books' positions; do not reconcile. Third-party screeners treat "Buyable Gap Ups" and "Buyable Gap Ups (ATR based)" as two distinct screens, which is consistent with this split.
  - **Internal conflict on porosity: 1-2% vs 2-3%.** Both published by the authors (1-2% in *Stocks that undercut the low of the gap up* and in the 6-day-rule FAQ; 2-3% in *What if a stock gaps up then some days later, gaps up again?* and in the secondary book review). Record both.
  - **Internal conflict on the stop hierarchy.** Book Rule #2 makes the gap-day intraday low the stop. The FAQ makes the **7-8% maximum loss** senior to it (*"you adhere to that rule first"*), and a further FAQ permits **9-10%**. Meanwhile Dr. K in a weekend review suggests the **10-day line** as *"a slightly more liberal stop"* than the BGU low. Four different stop authorities for one setup.
  - **Against O'Neil/IBD on the "Power Earnings Gap" / episodic pivot family**: third-party material treats BGU, Power Earnings Gap, and episodic pivot as near-synonyms. Morales/Kacher's BGU is the version with an explicit ATR gate and an explicit gap-day-low stop. `missing: fetched IBD/O'Neil and Minervini primaries in this session — no competing numbers are asserted here.`

## Ants / "Ants Breakout"

- **origin / source_name**: Morales & Kacher, VoSI report *PPR — Lyondellbasell Industries (LYB) Pocket Pivot Buy Point and "Ants Breakout"* (6 Nov 2010). The authors are explicit that the *nickname* is theirs: *"which look like 'ants' and so we have given them that nickname."* (The underlying up-12-of-15 measure is widely attributed elsewhere to David Ryan / O'Neil-lineage work; Morales/Kacher present it as a rule they use and annotate, and name the *breakout* variant.)

- **definition**: A dense cluster of up-days marking an accelerated advance, followed by a consolidation, whose subsequent breakout to new highs is the buy point. *"These little marks, which look like 'ants' and so we have given them that nickname, indicate that the stock is up 12 out of 15 days in a row or better at that point. We use this rule to buy the ensuing breakouts we call 'ants breakouts.'"*

- **criteria**:
  - the ants condition — **up 12 out of 15 days or better** — *"indicate that the stock is up 12 out of 15 days in a row or better at that point"* — **high**
  - the same threshold restated by a VoSI subscriber and not corrected by the authors — 12 of 15 — *"ants occur when a stock is up 12 out of 15 Days in a row or better"* — high (corroboration)
  - what makes it an **ants breakout** — the ants advance, then a consolidation, then a breakout to new highs — *"when a stock shows this kind of action where it is up 12 out of 15 days in a row or better and then backs up as it consolidates the immediately prior, very strong upside price move that produces the 'ants' on the way up, we then look for a breakout to new highs as another add/buy point for the stock."* — **high**
  - stop guide — the **20-day moving average** — *"we see LYB as buyable on both counts, using the 20-day moving average as a guide for a stop."* — high
  - market context increases aggression — value: null — *"Back in the old days, we would sometimes get very aggressive buying a stock on an 'ants breakout,' particularly if the general market is showing strength."* — high
  - author's ranking — ants sit **below** continuation pocket pivots in their preference — *"I would say I have slightly more comfort in buying continuation pocket pivots than 50d SMA bounces, 3 weeks tight, ants, and base breakouts."* — high

- **measured_performance**: none published. No win rate, no sample, no base rate.

- **invalidation**: not separately published. The stop is the 20-day moving average. The past-tense framing (*"Back in the old days"*) suggests the authors themselves de-emphasised aggressive ants-breakout buying, but no explicit retirement is stated.

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV): fully computable. `sum(close[i] > close[i-1] for i in last 15 trading days) >= 12`; then a consolidation detector; then `high[t] > max(high) over the consolidation` for the breakout; `SMA(close, 20)` as the stop. **Two ambiguities the source does not resolve**: (a) "up ... days in a row or better" is internally odd — 12 of 15 is not "in a row"; read it as 12 up-closes within a 15-day window. (b) The **consolidation** between the ants and the breakout has no published length or depth. `missing: the required consolidation duration/depth for an "ants breakout".` Note also this is the one setup here whose core condition is a *count of up-closes* rather than a volume comparison — cheap to compute and orthogonal to the pocket pivot.

- **conflicts**: none found on the 12-of-15 threshold; it is stated identically by the authors and by a VoSI subscriber. Attribution differs across the wider literature (David Ryan / IBD vs Morales-Kacher) but the *number* does not.

## Jesse Livermore's "Century Mark Rule" (long side, as Morales & Kacher apply it)

- **origin / source_name**: Jesse Livermore, via Edwin Lefèvre, *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator* — the authors cite **page 104 of the John A. Wiley & Sons edition published in 1994**. Dissected and modernised by Kacher & Morales in *Market Lab Report — Dissecting Jesse Livermore's Century Mark Rule*, Parts 1-6 (19 Apr - 6 May 2017). The rule is the "L" in their **OWL** framework (O'Neil-Wyckoff-Livermore).

- **definition**: Buy a stock as it crosses a "double-zero" round number (100, 200, 300…) **for the first time**, on the expectation of a rapid further advance. Livermore's own words, quoted by the authors: *"It was an old trading theory of mine that when a stock crossed 100 or 200 or 300 for the first time the price does not stop there but goes a good deal higher, so that if you buy it as soon as it crosses the line it is almost certain to show you a profit."*

- **criteria**:
  - trigger — first-ever cross of a century mark (100/200/300…) — verbatim as above — high
  - expected move — **over 13%**, generalised by the authors to **10-15% or more** — Livermore: *"I figured that when it crossed 300 it ought to keep going and probably touch 340 in a jiffy."* Authors: *"That implied a rapid movement of over 13% higher from the $300 price level, which we can call the 'point of impact.' From this we can surmise that based on his rule, a move of 10-15% or more was expected to occur 'in a jiffy.'"* — **high**
  - entry — as close to the century mark as possible — *"we might implement Livermore's rule with the idea of buying as close to the Century Mark in question for any stock"* — high
  - stop / downside porosity — **1-3% below the century mark** — *"This may argue for allowing for 1-3% of downside 'porosity' below the Century Mark before determining that the move has failed."* … *"then allowing for a downside stop that is 1-3% below that Century Mark."* — **high**
  - re-entry — explicitly permitted after a stop-out — *"one should remain flexible and open to the idea of re-entering the trade if the requisite condition re-materializes."* — high
  - universe filter added by the authors (not in Livermore) — **big-stock leaders only** — *"limiting our search for modern-day applications of Jesse Livermore's Century Mark Rule to current big-stock leaders might improve the profitability of this rule, assuming that it is valid in the first place."* — high. Note the authors' own hedge: *"assuming that it is valid in the first place."*
  - disqualifier — a gap that carries price far beyond the mark makes the entry unavailable — *"Because of how far beyond the $400 level it had moved on the gap after closing at 389.99 the day before, it was not possible to enter the stock near the 400 level as Livermore would prescribe."* — high

- **measured_performance**: **no aggregate statistic published.** Parts 1-6 are a **hand-picked case-study series with no denominator, no universe, and no failure count.** The individual figures they publish are:
  - NVDA, first cross of $100 (late 2016): *"That immediately led to a 20% move in just six days once the stock cleared the first Century Mark."*
  - ACIA, first cross of $100 (Aug 2016): *"ACIA hovered around the $100 level for another couple of days before launching 20% higher in just two days."*
  - TSLA, $100 (May 2013): *"The net move from the $100 price level was 14.9%, but the best one could have done based on where the stock traded was 14.6%, with something closer to 10-13% a more realistic outcome for a real-time trader."*
  - TSLA, $200 (Feb 2014): graded by the authors themselves as **partial**: *"this application of Jesse Livermore's Century Mark Rule gets partial credit for success."*
  - AMZN 1998: split-adjusted first $100 cross led to *"a 100% gain in 11 days"*; second $100 cross *"running up to 143.75 (a 43.75% move, obviously) in another two days"*; $200 cross *"quickly ran up 16% in two days"*; $300 cross *"it ran up 85% over the next two weeks."*
  - AMZN $400 (Dec 2012): an explicit **failure** — *"A Century Mark failure"*, the stock *"plummeting to the downside… over 100 points lower before it bottomed out at 284.38."*
  - **Base rate: none.** These are selected examples across four names; the authors never state how many century-mark crossings they examined or how many failed. Do not treat any of these percentages as an expectancy.

- **invalidation**: price falls more than 1-3% back below the century mark ⇒ the move has failed. A gap far above the mark ⇒ no valid entry. A century mark can act as resistance instead: *"a Century Mark can often serve as a major point of resistance for a stock."*

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV): computable, with caveats. Primitives: detect `high[t] >= K` where `K ∈ {100, 200, 300, …}` and `max(high) over all prior history < K` (first-ever cross — needs **full price history**, not a trailing window); stop at `K × (1 - 0.01…0.03)`; measure forward return over a short horizon for the 10-15% expectation.
  - **Not computable from a trailing window**: "for the first time" is an all-history condition.
  - **Split adjustment is a first-class hazard here and the authors say so.** Part 5 works entirely in split-adjusted vs nominal terms: *"Keep in mind that the prices shown along the right margin of the chart reflect post-split prices current as of 2017."* A century mark is a **nominal** price event; on a split-adjusted series the $100 line is at $8. **Any implementation must run on unadjusted prices or it will detect the wrong events.** This is the single most important implementation note in this file.
  - **NOT computable from OHLCV**: the "big-stock leader" universe filter the authors add.

- **conflicts**: Livermore's original claim (*"almost certain to show you a profit"*) is far stronger than the authors' own findings, which include an outright failure (AMZN at $400) and a "partial credit" case (TSLA at $200). The authors do not reconcile this and openly hedge (*"assuming that it is valid in the first place"*). Record both. No conflict with O'Neil/IBD or Minervini found — neither publishes a century-mark rule.

## Century Mark Rule "in Reverse" (short side)

- **origin / source_name**: Kacher & Morales, *Market Lab Report — Dissecting Jesse Livermore's Century Mark Rule — Part 6* (6 May 2017). Referenced by the Wiley chapter-3 metadata for *Short-Selling with the O'Neil Disciples* as *"Jesse Livermore's 'Century Mark Rule' in reverse"* — a newer short set-up.

- **criteria**:

  - the implied threshold for "failed to hold" — **more than 1-3% below the mark** (inherited from the long-side porosity in Part 4) — *"getting quickly stopped out by a move in excess of 3% below the Century Mark"* — **med** (Part 4 states this as the *long* stop; Part 6 does not restate a numeric trigger for the short side). `missing: an explicit short-side entry threshold below the century mark.`

- **detection_notes**: same all-history, **unadjusted-price** century-mark detector as the long side, plus a subsequent close back below `K` (or below `K × 0.97`). ## Wyckoff Undercut & Rally (U&R) / "Wyckoffian Retest" / the "Ugly Duckling"

- **origin / source_name**: Gil Morales (with Kacher), VoSI VooDoo(TM) Reports and FAQs. The authors attribute the underlying formation to **Richard D. Wyckoff** — it is the "W" in their **OWL** (O'Neil-Wyckoff-Livermore) framework, described as *"the surname initials of William O'Neil, Richard Wyckoff, and Jesse Livermore."* The U&R is *"derived from the work of Richard D. Wyckoff who referred to it as a 'spring' type of formation."*

- **definition**: Price undercuts a prior significant low in the pattern, washing out weak holders and attracting shorts, then rallies back **above** that low; the reclaim is the entry, with the undercut low as a very tight risk point. The authors' rationale, in Morales's own words (given for the short-side use of the same phenomenon, but it is the same mechanism): *"If a breakdown through a 'support' level is viewed by the crowd as 'bearish,' this is where natural sellers will be washed out of the stock and short-sellers will swarm the stock on the short side as they see the breach of support as indicative of further, impending downside. Thus, as everyone leans over to one side of the boat at the same time, selling is washed out in the short-term and the stock then rallies back up through the previously breached support level."* The **"Ugly Duckling"** is their name for the regime in which these work: *"Just when things look to be at maximum grimness for a particular stock, the Ugly Duckling shows up and sends the thing moving back to the upside."*

- **criteria**:
  - trigger — undercut a prior significant low, then rally back above it — *"undercut & rally entry points occur when a stock undercuts a prior significant low in its chart pattern then rallies above that low"* — med (rendered summary; the mechanism is stated primarily and identically elsewhere) / the questioner's restatement, uncorrected by the authors: *"finding a prior low in a pattern and then waiting till the current price drops below the prior low and buy it as it rallies back above"* — high
  - risk — **"a couple percent or less"** — *"Such patterns help keep risk to a minimum so your risk is just a couple percent or less should the market falter."* — med (rendered summary) — corroborated by the primary VDU statement *"your risk is typically less than 2-3%"* — high
  - selling guide — the undercut prior low itself, or the prior day's low — *"This becomes long entry using the 7.77 price low or yesterday's low at 8.03 as a selling guide."* (OKLO, 10 Jun 2024) — **high**. Note the authors offer **two** stop anchors for one entry.
  - the **Wyckoffian retest / test for supply** component — a low-volume retest that turns — *"Here we see two lows on heavy volume, where the second low results in a price U&R coming up through the prior 7.77 low, followed by a low-volume test that is trying to turn."* — **high**. Note the volume shape: **heavy volume on the lows, light volume on the retest.**
  - best context — leading stocks, coinciding with a market turn off the lows — *"They tend to work best in leading stocks, and usually coincide with a market pullback or correction where the market bottoms and turns back to the upside at the same time. That is not always the case, however, but I've found that the highest velocity to the upside is usually seen when they occur in conjunction with a market turn off the lows."* — high
  - a base-breakout failure is **not automatically** a short in this regime — *"in this market a base-breakout failure generally turns out to provide a short-term short-sale target as it in fact sets about building a new base."* — high

- **measured_performance**: none published. "Work best," "highest velocity," "typically less than 2-3%" risk — all asserted, no sample, no win rate, no base rate.

- **invalidation**: the stock fails to reclaim the undercut low, or breaks back below it (the stop). No further published invalidation.

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV): the core is computable. Primitives: pivot-low detection over a lookback (a "prior significant low" — **the source never defines significance**, so this is your parameter); `low[t] < prior_low` on some day; then `close[t+k] > prior_low` for the reclaim; stop at `prior_low` or `low[t+k-1]`. The Wyckoffian-retest refinement adds a volume shape: high volume at the two lows, **volume dry-up** on the retest — computable as `volume` vs `SMA(volume, 50)`.
  - `missing: a definition of "prior significant low" — swing-low order, minimum depth, and lookback are all unpublished. This single undefined parameter dominates the signal count.`
  - **NOT computable from daily OHLCV alone**: the general-market-turn condition (needs index data — computable, but not from the stock's own bars), "leading stocks," and the "Ugly Duckling" regime judgement.

- **conflicts**: **Direct conflict with the pocket pivot's own Ten Rules.** Rule #8 forbids buying a pocket pivot where the stock *"sells off hard down through the 10-dma or 50-dma then shoots straight back up"* — a "V". The U&R/Ugly Duckling is, structurally, a purchase of exactly that kind of sharp reversal off a washout low. Both are Morales/Kacher. The authors do not reconcile these; the U&R material is largely later (2017-2024) than the Ten Rules, and the *Six Ways* report frames the shift as regime-driven (*"today's markets… are far more unforgiving thus we have counseled members to buy on constructive weakness as opposed to strength"*). Record both; do not average.

## "Voodoo" / Volume Dry-Up (VDU) Pullback

- **origin / source_name**: Gil Morales & Chris Kacher, VoSI — trademarked as **VoSI VooDoo(TM)** report series. *Market Lab Report EXCLUSIVE — Six Ways to Boost Your Trading Profits* (1 Mar 2017); weekend PP/BGU reviews; ongoing *Pocket Pivot Review/Volume Dry-Up* reports through Dec 2025.

- **definition**: A pullback into support on extremely low volume, bought close to the support line so risk is tiny. *"At the heart of this phenomenon on a granular level is the concept of 'voodoo' volume dry-ups in a stock that has previously broken down."* … *"The beauty of VDU pullbacks is that the stock can be bought when it's very close to support so your risk is typically less than 2-3%."*

- **criteria**:
  - volume condition — **value: null** — no threshold is published. The one worked example: *"Volume on Friday was particularly light at -71.4% below average, which qualifies as a 'voodoo' day, or an extreme volume dry-up (VDU)."* — high for the *example*, but **-71.4% is a measurement of one day, not a stated rule.** `missing: the published percentage-below-average-volume threshold at which a day "qualifies" as a voodoo day / VDU. Do NOT adopt -71.4% as the threshold — it is an observation, and the sentence describes it as "particularly light" and "extreme", implying it is well beyond the minimum.`
  - denominator for "below average" — **value: null** — the report says "below average" without naming the averaging period. `missing: whether "average" means the 50-day average volume or another window.`
  - support reference — a moving average or a nearby prior low — *"TTD and Z have both pulled back into their 50-day moving averages, which would put them in lower-risk entry positions using the 50-day lines as tight selling guides."* and, for the 10-dma case, *"pulled down into its 10-day moving average"* — high
  - risk — **less than 2-3%**, with a worked case at **-1.7%** — *"you could have bought on Dec 9, 2016 then sold if it undercut the low of Dec 8, 2016, or a risk of just -1.7% on the trade."* — high
  - sequencing vs the pocket pivot — the VDU comes **first** and is the better entry; the pocket pivot that follows is often too late — *"In such cases, pocket pivots are often too late to be buying as the risk noticeable increases."* — **high**. This is a significant operational claim: the authors say their own flagship signal is frequently the *worse* of the two entries.

- **measured_performance**: none published. Two single-name anecdotes, n=1 each: TTD *"peaking at a potential profit of +14.4%"* off the Dec 2016 VDU, and *"TTD proceeded to move higher by +15.1% from its 50-day line"* over the following weeks. Both are hindsight peak-to-entry measurements with **no exit rule applied**, and the report itself concedes *"of course, no one sells at the top."* No base rate. Not usable as expectancy.

- **invalidation**: undercut of the prior day's low (the stated stop in the TTD example). Otherwise unpublished.

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV): the *shape* is computable — `volume[t] << SMA(volume, N)` while price sits within some distance of `SMA(close,10)` or `SMA(close,50)` — but **the threshold and the averaging window are both unpublished**, so any VDU screen is parameterised by you, not by the authors. Record it that way. The risk measurement (`entry / prior_day_low - 1`) is exactly computable.

- **conflicts**: **The authors rank the VDU above their own pocket pivot as an entry** (*"pocket pivots are often too late to be buying as the risk noticeable increases"*), which sits against the pocket pivot's original selling point of being the *early* entry. Both claims are theirs. Also note the general shift the *Six Ways* report states plainly and which conflicts with the Ten Rules' strength-buying orientation: *"buy on constructive weakness as opposed to strength."*

## Late-Stage Failed-Base (LSFB)

- **origin / source_name**: Gil Morales & Chris Kacher. *"first formally discussed in the book Trade Like an O'Neil Disciple (Wiley, 2010)"*; treated at length in *Short-Selling with the O'Neil Disciples: Turn to the Dark Side of Trading* (Wiley) — Morales: *"This is the 'late-stage failed-base' or LSFB short-sale set-up that is discussed in the most recent short-selling book."* Applied in VoSI SSS (Short-Sale Set-up) reports.

- **definition**: A leading stock forms a **late-stage** base (i.e. late in its overall advance), breaks out, and the breakout **fails**; the failure and the subsequent weak drift back toward the failed breakout point is the short entry. It is the **earliest** of their short set-ups — it can fire within days of the peak, before a top has had time to build.

- **criteria**:
  - timing relative to the peak — **within days of the peak**, and this is the explicit exception to their 8-12-week rule — *"Seek to short former, big-stock leaders 8-12 weeks after their peak price following a major upside price run, except when dealing with potential late-stage breakout failures that lead to immediate Late-Stage Failed-Base (LSFB) short-sale set-ups."* — **high**
  - what constitutes the set-up — one or more breakout failures — *"Berry Plastics Group (BERY) became a late-stage failed-base (LSFB) short-sale set-up after two breakout failures at the end of January and early February."* — high
  - characteristic post-failure behaviour — sideways drift back up to the failed breakout point — *"The stock then trended sideways as it drifted back up to the prior base breakout point in early March. This is in fact typical action for an LSFB short-sale set-up."* — **high**
  - optimal short entry — a weak rally into moving-average resistance — *"The stock rallied weakly into the confluence of its 10-day, 50-day and 20-dema lines on Friday. This puts it in an optimal short-sale position using the moving averages as a guide for a tight upside stop."* — high
  - alternative (wider) stop — the failed breakout price itself — *"Alternatively, a wider stop could be employed by using the prior 51.75 base breakout point at which the stock initially failed."* — high
  - the trigger in the JAZZ example — a breach of the 50-dma — *"a breach of the 50-dma is a clear short-sale signal, although one could take an initial position here in anticipation of such a break, with the idea of getting more aggressive if the break is confirmed."* — high
  - stop in the JAZZ example — **3-5%** — *"Use a standard 3-5% upside stop to keep risk under control."* — **high**
  - the moving averages they name for LSFB resistance — **10-dma, 20-dema, 50-dma** — as quoted above — high
  - LSFBs are the **first wave** of short candidates in a topping market — *"Look for Late-Stage Failed-Base Set-Ups as the First Wave of Short-Sale Candidates in a Potentially Early Market Correction or Bear Market Phase"* — high
  - source of candidates — your own prior buy list — *"your initial list of short-sale target candidates will actually come from your prior buy watch list as leading stocks start to break down"* … *"the typical 'pile in, pile out' principle that is the backbone of our short-selling methods."* — high

- **measured_performance**: none published. Named examples only (BERY, NVDA, CAT, JAZZ, AAOI, LITE, BZUN, COHR, MOMO) with no outcomes, no sample, no base rate.

- **invalidation**: the stock reclaims the failed breakout point — *"the prior breakout failure remains in force since the stock has not been able to get back above the breakout price point just a few cents below 170."* Explicit acknowledgement of failure risk: *"There is some risk that the stock will break out again."* Upside stop 3-5% or the nearest moving average.

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV): requires a **base detector plus a base-stage counter** — "late-stage" is the load-bearing word and it is a *count of bases since the advance began*, which is not derivable from bars without a base-sequencing layer. Once you have that: detect breakout (`close > base_high`), detect failure (`close` back below `base_high`, or below the 50-dma), then a weak rally into `SMA(close,10)`/`EMA(close,20)`/`SMA(close,50)` confluence as the entry.
  - **NOT computable from daily OHLCV alone**: base *stage* numbering (needs the full prior advance segmented into bases), "big-stock leader" status, "weak" rally (they mean low volume — computable as a proxy), and general-market phase.
  - `missing: a published definition of "late-stage" — no base count (e.g. "third-stage or later") is stated in any fetched source.`

- **conflicts**: **The LSFB is an explicit exception to their own Rule #3.** Rule #3 says short 8-12 weeks after the peak; the LSFB says *"can present a short-sale entry within days after a leading stock's peak price occurs."* The authors state this as a carve-out rather than a contradiction, but it means the timing rule has two incompatible values (8-12 weeks vs days) depending on a pattern classification you must make first. Record both.
  Also note a **source-internal citation discrepancy**: Part 5 of the Six Rules series says *Short-Selling with the O'Neil Disciples* was *"published by John Wiley & Sons in April 2014"*, while the book's ISBN listing (9781118970973) and other references date it **2015**. Recorded, not reconciled.

## Head & Shoulders (H&S), incl. the "fractal" H&S and the "big head" H&S

- **origin / source_name**: Gil Morales & Chris Kacher, *Short-Selling with the O'Neil Disciples* ch. 3 (*Short-Selling Set-Ups: Chart Patterns for the Dark Side* — Wiley describes it as the *"textbook"* pattern of the three); Morales also co-authored *How to Make Money Selling Stocks Short* with William O'Neil (2004). Applied in VoSI SSS reports. The **"fractal" H&S** and **"big head" H&S** are their own named variants.

- **definition**: The classical H&S top, traded from the **right shoulder peak** rather than only from the neckline break. Their variants: a **"fractal" H&S** is a small H&S visible on the daily chart nested inside a larger topping structure on the weekly (*"With the stock now under the 50-dma in what appears to be a 'fractal' head and shoulders formation"* … *"The 'fractal' H&S formation that we see on the daily chart is a typical topping formation"*); a **"big head" head and shoulders** is what a POD morphs into (*"Short sale POD topping formations can also morph into other types of topping formations such as what I like to call the 'big head' head and shoulders formation."*).

- **criteria**:
  - preferred entry — at or near the **right shoulder peak**, not the neckline break — *"Carbo Ceramics (CRR) looks like it may be at the peak of a right shoulder within a head and shoulders top formation. It appears shortable at current levels…"* — **high**
  - the moving average used at the right shoulder in that example — the **65-day exponential moving average** — *"It appears shortable at current levels, just below the 65-day exponential moving average at 150.57, which could be used as a tight upside stop."* — **high**. (Note: the 65-dema is a distinctive Morales/Kacher line, not the 10/20/50/200 set they usually cite.)
  - alternative entry — a gap-down through the neckline, with the **neckline as the stop** — *"the stock has gapped down below the neckline of an Head and Shoulders topping formation. The stock is shortable here using the neckline, around 290, as your upside stop."* — high
  - the **optimal** entry after a neckline break is a rally back **into** the neckline — *"Ideally, a rally back up into the neckline would be the most optimal place to short the stock, but that may not happen if the general market reverses today."* — **high**
  - downside objective — the prior lows — *"The initial downside price objective if this works would be the prior lows at around 120."* (CRR, from ~150 ⇒ ~20%) — high for the *method* (project to prior lows), and consistent with the published 20-30% profit target in Rule #6
  - the common precondition with the LSFB — *"Both patterns rely on 'high-volume break off of the peak'"* — high
  - intraday entry timing on a rally — resistance confluence plus weak volume — *"If the market is rallying on weak volume… then we look at our target short-sale stocks to see if this is coinciding with them moving up into a logical area of resistance, such as an area of prior overhead supply, or the 50-day, 20-day or 200-day moving averages. I then use 20 cent upside stops on short positions as I test and probe the rally for weakness."* — **high** (the **20 cent** stop is exact but is a probe-size tactic, not a pattern rule)

- **measured_performance**: none published. Named examples only (CRR, NFLX, FOSL, LULU, CELG, SSYS, V, NTAP, SWKS, TSLA, FSLR, BRCM), no outcomes tabulated, no sample, no base rate.

- **invalidation**: price back above the neckline (when the neckline is the stop) or above the moving average used at the right shoulder. General short-side stop: 3-5% (Rule #5).

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV): H&S detection needs a swing-point extractor (three peaks with the middle highest, two intervening troughs defining a neckline) — standard but parameter-heavy, and **none of those parameters are published by the authors**. What *is* directly computable from their material: `EMA(close, 65)` as a right-shoulder resistance test; `SMA(close, 50)`, `EMA(close, 20)`, `SMA(close, 200)` as rally-resistance tests; volume on the break off the peak vs `SMA(volume, 50)`.
  - **NOT computable from daily OHLCV alone**: the "fractal" classification (requires simultaneous daily *and* weekly structure — computable if you resample, but it is a two-timeframe test, not a single-series one); "areas of prior overhead supply"; and the general-market-break requirement.
  - `missing: published shoulder-symmetry, neckline-slope, and duration parameters. Every H&S geometry parameter in an implementation is yours.`

- **conflicts**: **Against the textbook/classical H&S**, which triggers on the **neckline break**. Morales/Kacher prefer the earlier, riskier right-shoulder entry, and treat the neckline break as a *second-best* entry whose *optimal* form is the rally back into the neckline. Record both. No numeric conflict with O'Neil is recorded here — Morales co-wrote the 2004 O'Neil short-selling book, and the later book is described as *"an updated version using daily charts"*, implying a **timeframe change (weekly → daily)** rather than a rule change. `missing: the 2004 O'Neil/Morales book's own parameters, not fetched in this session.`

## Punchbowl of Death (POD)

- **origin / source_name**: Dr. Chris Kacher (first observed) with Gil Morales — VoSI article *The Punchbowl of Death (POD) Short-Sale Set-Up*, bylined *"By Dr. Chris Kacher and Gil Morales"*. Treated in *Short-Selling with the O'Neil Disciples* ch. 3, where Wiley's own description calls it the *"esoteric"* topping formation of the three. Kacher: *"an interesting short-sale set-up or formation that I first observed in the 1990's."*

- **definition**: A giant double-top shaped like an over-sized cup — a "punchbowl" — in a former hot leader. Four movements, **labelled A-D by the authors**:
  - **A** — *"the initial 'hot stock' price run that usually creates a great deal of excitement and which gets ever more obvious as the stock continues to run ever higher"*, ending at high **H1**.
  - **B** — a sharp break down. *"This is the initial characteristic of POD formation: a sharp decline off the peak after a leading stock has had an accelerated upside move."*
  - **C** — *"a short bottoming process and then another sharp move to the upside that approaches or exceeds the prior high on the left side of the giant 'bowl' or POD formation"*, ending at **H2**.
  - **D** — the final breakdown.
  The naming is theirs: *"these big, double-top type set-ups… resemble massive, over-sized 'cup' formations. But nobody drinks from cups that big, hence the term 'punchbowl.'"*

- **criteria**:
  - H2 need **not** exceed H1 — *"This is a typical feature of POD formations, although the H2 high does not always have to be higher than H1 – sometimes they can be roughly the same or H2 can be lower than H1."* — **high**
  - depth of the B decline — **no single published threshold**; the authors give worked examples: ARBA *"corrected over 70% from its March 2000 peak"*; Syntex *"plummeted nearly 75% off its highs"*; Simmonds Precision *"a nearly 50% deep 'cup'"* — value: null — `missing: a stated minimum depth for the B leg. The 50-75% range is a set of examples, not a rule.`
  - duration, long-term POD — **about a year or more** — *"the AOL/TWX and SCHW examples are longer-term PODs that take about a year or more to form"* — **high**
  - duration, hot-IPO POD — **about three months** — *"This whole POD took about three months to form"* (LDK Solar) — **high**
  - duration, narrow POD — **18 weeks** — *"Note that right off the peak in early January JASO took 18 weeks to form the entire pattern and then fail from that point."* — **high**
  - narrowness is preferred — *"Oftentimes the best PODs to short on the right side of the pattern are those that are the narrowest."* — high
  - IPO window for the short-term variant — **within 1-2 years** of coming public — *"Short selling POD patterns in hot IPOs or recent new issues (within 1-2 years)"* — high
  - **entry trigger** — a break below the **10-week** (weekly) / **50-day** (daily) moving average on the right side — *"once the stocks break down below the 10-week, or its daily equivalent in the 50-day, moving average on the right side of the formation that is the general point at which the stocks should be shorted."* — **high**
  - **stop** — the same 10-week / 50-day line — *"Using the 10-week or 50-day moving average at that point also serves as a ready guide for your upside stop."* — **high**
  - **market-timing requirement** — the breakdown must coincide with a market down leg — *"the breakdown off the right peak of any POD will almost always coincide with a market down leg. This is an important short-selling tip, which is that short-sale target stocks must always be handled and shorted in conjunction with a downside market break where the odds of success are much higher."* — **high**
  - when the C leg occurs — in a secondary bull leg or a bear-market rally — *"these 'C' leg rallies that complete the right side of the POD in big former leaders usually occur in secondary bull market legs or bear market rallies – that is a very important concept to understand about PODs."* — **high**
  - persistence required — the first break of the line often fails — *"would-be POD short-sellers should also keep in mind that this can require some persistence. Note how in the AAPL example, above, the stock starts to break down below the blue 10-week line a few times, even finding resistance at the line in the May-June 2008 time period, and then rallying above it one more time in July 2008 before it finally rolls over."* — high
  - a **"double POD"** variant exists — SCHW *"actually formed a 'double POD' type of formation, making one more attempt at is highs on H3"* — high
  - a **"slow stock" POD** variant exists — IGT 2008 — high

- **measured_performance**: **none published.** The article is entirely a catalogue of named historical examples: Syntex (1962-66), Simmonds Precision (1964-66), Reading Railroad (1907), AOL/TWX, SCHW, ARBA, TASR, AMZN, DRYS, LDK, JASO, IGT, FSLR, BRCM, AAPL, BIDU, GOOG. **No win rate, no sample size, no failure count, no base rate.** The strongest claim is qualitative: *"If played correctly, short sale set-up POD formations can offer some of the most rewarding downside short-sale plays, but timing is critical."* Note the survivorship exposure: every example is a POD that worked, selected after the fact.

- **invalidation**: price back above the 10-week / 50-day line (the stop). The authors explicitly warn the pattern can fail repeatedly before working (the AAPL persistence note), and a POD can *morph* into a "big head" H&S rather than break down directly. **The market-context condition is effectively an invalidator**: shorting a POD breakdown without a coincident market down leg is, by their own statement, the low-odds version of the trade.

- **detection_notes** (daily/weekly OHLCV):
  - Primitives: a prior advance (leg A) with a peak H1; a decline of some depth (**threshold unpublished — 50-75% observed**); a recovery to within some tolerance of H1 (**tolerance unpublished — H2 may be above, equal to, or below H1**); then `close < SMA(close,50)` daily or `close < SMA(close,10)` weekly as the trigger; stop at that same line.
  - **The two undefined parameters — B-leg depth and H2-vs-H1 tolerance — are what separate a POD from an ordinary cup base.** Without them, a POD detector and a cup-with-handle detector fire on the same bars. The authors are explicit that the shape *is* a cup (*"it tries to form what is really an improper, big, cup-with-handle"*, *"a big POD-with-handle type of formation"*), so **the long-side cup pattern and the short-side POD are the same geometry distinguished only by stage, depth, and market context.** Flag this loudly for the screener: a POD is a *cup you should short*.
  - Duration is computable and usefully wide: ~18 weeks to a year+.
  - **NOT computable from daily OHLCV alone**: "hot stock"/"big former leader" status; where the general market is in its cycle (needs index data + a phase classifier); the IPO date for the 1-2-year new-issue window; and the distinction between a bear-market rally and a secondary bull leg for the C-leg condition.

- **conflicts**: **The POD directly inverts the O'Neil long-side cup-with-handle.** The same visual structure that O'Neil-style buyers treat as a base, Kacher/Morales treat — when it is oversized, late in a leader's life, and preceded by a 50-75% break — as the premier topping formation. Both readings come from the O'Neil lineage. Record both; do not reconcile. No numeric conflict with Minervini or IBD is recorded (neither publishes a POD).

## The Six Essential Rules of Short-Selling (the numeric framework the short patterns sit inside)

- **origin / source_name**: Gil Morales (with edits by Dr. Chris Kacher), VoSI Market Lab Report series Parts 1-6 (27 Jul - 17 Aug 2017), explicitly excerpted from the book: *"Portions of this article have been excerpted from 'Short-Selling with the O'Neil Disciples: Turn to the Dark Side of Trading' by Gil Morales & Chris Kacher, published by John Wiley & Sons in April 2014."* Wiley's chapter metadata confirms *"six rules dealing with cycle timing, stock selection (in terms of price action and volume), trade timing, and setting stops and profit targets."*

- **definition**: The wrapper rules that govern when the H&S / LSFB / POD patterns may be traded, and how they are sized, stopped, and exited.

- **criteria**:
  - **Rule #1 — cycle timing.** No number for the rule itself, but a published correction band: normal corrections of **7-12%** are tradeable on the short side — *"Normal market corrections of 7-12% during an overall bull market cycle can offer decent opportunities on the short side."* and *"Often, these tops will coincide with shorter-term market corrections of 7-12%."* — **high**. Morales explicitly retracts the older O'Neil-era teaching: *"both I and Bill O'Neil stipulated that short-selling should only be undertaken during a bear market… In hindsight… I can safely say that this is woefully simplistic."* — high
  - **Rule #2 — stock selection.** Big-stock leaders showing topping signs — *"Focus on those 'big stock' leaders that had huge upside price moves in the immediately preceding bull market phase and that are showing significant signs of topping."* — high. Supporting figures: institutional accumulation *"3-5 years typical"*; P/E expansion *"from 30-40x earnings to double or triple that during uptrends"* — med (these are rendered from the report body; the P/E figures are descriptive, not screening thresholds). Entry signal: *"High-volume break off of the peak."* — high
  - **Rule #3 — trade timing.** **8-12 weeks after the peak**, with the LSFB exception — *"Seek to short former, big-stock leaders 8-12 weeks after their peak price following a major upside price run, except when dealing with potential late-stage breakout failures that lead to immediate Late-Stage Failed-Base (LSFB) short-sale set-ups."* — **high**
  - **Rule #4 — liquidity.** **Minimum 1-2 million shares/day** — *"Only sell short stocks that trade a minimum of 1-2 million shares a day, and preferably more."* — **high**. *"2-5 million preferred"* — med.
  - **Rule #5 — stops.** **3-5%, using 3% if the stock rallies against you on strong above-average volume**; optional layering at **3% / 5% / 7%** in thirds — *"Set stop-losses at an average of 3-5%, using the tighter 3% stop if the stock begins to rally against you on strong, above-average volume. A 'layering' technique can also be used by 'peeling off' and covering portions of the position as the stock rallies against you at a series of specified percentage thresholds, such as covering 1/3rd at 3%, another 1/3rd at 5%, and the final 1/3rd at 7%, or any other permutations."* — **high**. Portfolio-level alternative: **1% of total account value** — *"I won't let a short position turn into more than a 1% loss to my overall portfolio."* — high. MA-based alternative: *"if it feels soft, I might give it 2-3% beyond a moving average"* — high.
  - **Rule #6 — profit targets.** **20-30%**, or layered **15-20% / 20-30%** in halves — *"Once a short position is showing a profit, set a downside profit target objective of 20-30% or use a 'layering' technique, covering ½ the position once a 15-20% profit is showing, and the other ½ at a 20-30% profit. Alternatively, once a short position is showing a good profit one can use the 20-day moving average as a guide for a trailing stop. If the nearest moving average above the stock's current price is the 50-day or 200-day moving average then that can be used in place of the 20-day moving average line."* — **high**. Practical caveat published alongside: *"sometimes the downside move is more within the 10-20% range, and using the 'undercut & rally' method is generally more accurate."* — high

- **measured_performance**: none published for the rule set. One unaudited personal claim about the author: *"Mr. Morales achieved roughly triple digit percentage gains at times such as in 2001 and in the flash crash of 2010."* No sample, no period definition beyond two named events, no base rate, no verification. Treat as a marketing claim, not a measurement.

- **invalidation**: Rule #5 is the invalidation layer for every short pattern above: 3-5% against you, or 1% of portfolio.

- **detection_notes**: Rules #4 (`SMA(volume,50) >= 1_000_000`), #3 (weeks since the peak high), #5 and #6 (percentage stops and targets) are all directly computable from daily OHLCV plus a peak detector. Rule #1 requires index data and a market-phase classifier. Rule #2 requires fundamentals (P/E, institutional ownership) — **not computable from OHLCV**.

- **conflicts**: **Morales explicitly contradicts his own earlier co-authored position with William O'Neil** — that shorting should happen only in a bear market, near its start. He calls that *"woefully simplistic"* and broadens it to 7-12% corrections within bull markets. Record both: O'Neil/Morales 2004 = bear markets only; Morales/Kacher 2015 = also 7-12% bull-market corrections. Also note the **Rule #3 vs LSFB** timing conflict recorded in the LSFB section (8-12 weeks vs days).

## The 7-Week Rule and the "violation" sell rule (the exit that governs PP and BGU)

- **origin / source_name**: Dr. Chris Kacher ("Dr. K's 7-week rule") & Gil Morales, *Trade Like an O'Neil Disciple* and VoSI FAQs. Included here because both the pocket pivot and the BGU are only fully specified with it, and because it carries exact numbers.

- **definition**: Which moving average you may use as a trailing sell guide depends on whether the stock has respected the 10-dma for at least 7 weeks since the buy point.

- **criteria**:
  - the threshold — **7 weeks** — *"If the stock obeys the 10dma for at least 7 weeks, then we use the 10dma to sell the stock if it violates it."* … *"a stock must have not violated its 10dma for a minimum of 7 weeks to use the 10dma as your sell guide."* — **high**
  - if the stock violates the 10-dma inside 7 weeks — switch to the **50-dma** — *"If the stock does not obey the 10dma for at least 7 weeks, we switch to using the 50dma as our sell guide to avoid getting whipped out of the position."* and *"if you are showing a profit and the stock violates its 10dma in 7 weeks or less, you should switch to using a violation of the 50dma as your selling guide."* — **high**
  - definition of a **violation** — two-step, exact and computable — *"A violation occurs when the stock closes below the 10dma, then in subsequent days, trades below the low of that day."* — **high**
  - a second violation can reset the stop higher — *"The second break can reset your stop higher to the low of the second break but it is contextual to the overall chart."* — high
  - where the 7-week count starts — contextual; from the pivot day if the stock was basing — *"In cases where the stock has been basing, the count generally begins on the day of the pivot (pocket pivot, breakout, gap up, etc), since a basing stock is highly unlikely to obey its 10dma for more than a couple weeks."* — **high**
  - clock resets — **BGU: yes; in-base pocket pivot / base breakout: usually; continuation pocket pivot: no** — *"It would be reset for a buyable gap up. It would usually be reset for a pocket pivot within a base or coinciding with a base breakout, though it is contextual. In some special cases, the count may begin a few days or more prior to the pocket pivot. It would not be reset for a continuation (follow on) pocket pivot."* — **high**
  - post-7-week sell trigger — *"A pocket pivot buy point which results in an uptrend that is shown to obey the 10-day SMA for at least 7 weeks following the initial pocket pivot should be sold upon its initial 'violation' of the 10-day SMA."* — med (secondary restatement; the primary FAQs state the same rule in the authors' words)
  - hard override — the 7-8% maximum loss is senior — *"Most investors use a 7-8% maximum stop loss. Always adhere to that first."* — high

- **measured_performance**: none published. One anecdote: NVDA *"is also a name that could have been held invoking Dr. K's 7-week rule as its price never got ahead of itself for the first several weeks after these buy points emerged."*

- **invalidation**: the violation definition above.

- **detection_notes** (daily OHLCV): **fully computable and unusually precisely specified** — this is the cleanest rule in the whole Morales/Kacher corpus. State machine: on the buy-point day start a clock; each day check `close < SMA(close,10)`; if so, arm a pending violation at that day's `low`; a violation fires when a later day trades below that low. Track weeks since the buy point; if a violation fires at `< 7 weeks`, switch the sell guide to `SMA(close,50)` and re-arm the same two-step logic against the 50-dma. Reset the clock on a BGU or an in-base pocket pivot, but **not** on a continuation pocket pivot.
  - The only non-computable part is the *"contextual"* start of the count and the "special cases" where it begins earlier.

- **conflicts**: The FAQ *Using 10-dma vs 20-dema* offers the **20-dema** as a substitute for the 10-dma when a stock repeatedly slices the 10-day line — which changes the very line the 7-week rule is defined against, with **no restated week count** for the 20-dema variant. `missing: whether the 7-week threshold still applies when the 20-dema is substituted.` Record as an open ambiguity, not a resolved rule.

## Cross-cutting notes for the screener

1. **The pocket pivot's volume primitive is unlike every other volume rule in this corpus.** It compares today's volume to the **maximum down-close-day volume in a trailing 10-day window** — not to an average, and not to a percentile. A single anomalous distribution day legitimately suppresses signals for up to 10 sessions, and the authors say to accept that (*"It is best to use a purist approach and avoid the stock"*) — while elsewhere permitting discretionary exclusion of that day (*"you could disregard this as a red volume day"*). The rule is therefore **deterministic as published and discretionary as practised.** Implement the deterministic form; do not implement the discretionary escape.

2. **Two of their headline numbers are unresolvable from the sources I could reach.** The BGU volume gate ("1.5 times" vs "150% above" the 50-day average — a 1.67× difference) and the VDU threshold (no number published at all). Both are marked `value: null` / `missing:` above. Neither should ship as a Morales/Kacher-attributed rule until the book text is read directly.

3. **The Century Mark Rule must run on unadjusted prices.** It is the only setup in this file whose trigger is a *nominal* price level. The authors work through this explicitly in Part 5. A split-adjusted feed will silently detect the wrong events, and the failure is invisible — it produces plausible-looking signals at the wrong bars.

4. **Long and short geometries collide.** The POD is an oversized cup; the U&R is a "V" the Ten Rules tell you to avoid; the LSFB is a base breakout that failed. In each case the *same* bars support opposite trades and the discriminator is **stage + market context**, neither of which is computable from a single stock's OHLCV. Any implementation must make its stage and market-phase inputs explicit rather than burying them.

5. **Nothing in this corpus carries a measured win rate with a stated sample.** The single quantitative performance claim — *"about half of high quality stocks showing pocket pivots will work"* — has no sample size, no period, no universe, and its only stated benchmark is itself unquantified. Every other number in this file is a **rule parameter** (a threshold, a stop, a lookback), not a **result**. Treat the whole body as asserted structure, not validated edge.
